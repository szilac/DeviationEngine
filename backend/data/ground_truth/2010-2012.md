# Ground-Truth World-State Report: 2010 to 2012

## I. Executive Summary

**Political shifts.** The largest political event of the period was the wave of popular uprisings across Arabic-speaking states. It began with the self-immolation of Mohamed Bouazizi in Sidi Bouzid, Tunisia, in December 2010.
- Zine El Abidine Ben Ali left Tunisia on 14 January 2011.
- Hosni Mubarak resigned in Egypt on 11 February 2011.
- Muammar Gaddafi's rule in Libya collapsed after an armed revolt, backed by NATO air power under a UN Security Council mandate, and he was killed in October 2011.
- Yemen's Ali Abdullah Saleh signed a Gulf Cooperation Council transition deal on 23 November 2011. His vice-president, Abd Rabbuh Mansur Hadi, was then elected as the only candidate in February 2012.
- In Syria, protests that began in March 2011 turned into a multi-front armed conflict.

Elsewhere, leaders changed through ordinary procedure, and several of these changes were among the most important of the period:
- In China, the Communist Party's 18th Congress (November 2012) made Xi Jinping General Secretary and cut the Politburo Standing Committee from nine members to seven. This came after the expulsion of Chongqing party chief Bo Xilai, the party's most serious leadership crisis in years.
- Vladimir Putin returned to the Russian presidency in March 2012 with an official 63.6% of the vote, after the largest Moscow street protests since the 1990s.
- Barack Obama was re-elected in the United States in November 2012.
- Japan's Democratic Party government was heading into a December 2012 general election, having had three prime ministers in the period.

**Economic changes.** The world was still working through the aftermath of the 2008–09 financial crisis:
- Large fiscal and credit stimulus in China and much of the emerging world drove a strong rebound in 2010. Brazil grew 7.5% and China above 10% in official data.
- Growth then slowed in 2011–12. In Europe, sovereign debt problems spread from Greece to Ireland, Portugal, Spain, Italy and Cyprus.
- Commodity prices were high, especially for food and oil. This moved income toward exporters in the Gulf, Russia, Australia, Latin America and parts of Africa, and hurt poor food-importing households.

**Social developments.** Mass mobilisation was widespread and often organised through mobile phones and social media:
- Tahrir Square in Egypt.
- Spain's 15-M "indignados".
- Occupy Wall Street in the United States.
- Chile's student movement.
- India's anti-corruption campaign led by Anna Hazare.
- Russia's "For Fair Elections" rallies.
- The anti-Japanese demonstrations of August–September 2012 in more than 100 Chinese cities.

The protests had different causes but shared an urban, young and digitally connected base.

**Knowledge and technique.**
- Smartphones and mobile internet spread quickly across Asia, Africa and Latin America. So did mobile money, notably M-Pesa in Kenya.
- China's space and high-speed-rail programmes grew. So did India's Aadhaar biometric identity scheme.
- In physics, CERN announced in July 2012 the discovery of a Higgs-like boson.
- Hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling were raising US oil and gas output, which the IMF discussed in a box on "unconventional energy" in its October 2012 World Economic Outlook.

**Crises.** The period had an unusually dense run of disasters:
- The Haiti earthquake (January 2010).
- Russia's heat wave and grain export ban (2010).
- Pakistan's floods (2010).
- Japan's March 2011 earthquake, tsunami and Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident.
- Famine in southern Somalia (2011).
- A food crisis in the Sahel (2012).
- The Thai floods (2011).
- India's grid collapse (July 2012).

**Overall characterisation.** Seen from the end of 2012, the world was multipolar in economic weight but still largely unipolar in military reach. Its institutions (the G20, the IMF, the ECB, the UN Security Council) were being tested by crises they had not been built for. Many of its regimes, from Cairo to Moscow to Beijing, faced new kinds of public pressure, and the outcomes were genuinely undetermined.

## II. Global Overview

### Distribution of power

**Population and production.** The period's own statistics place the world's population and a large and growing share of its production in Asia:
- China's sixth national census (zero hour 1 November 2010) counted 1,339,724,852 people in the mainland.
- India's 2011 census provisional totals, released 31 March 2011, counted 1,210,193,422.
- Together the two held well over a third of humanity.
- China was the largest goods exporter and manufacturer, and the largest consumer of many industrial commodities.

**The United States.** It remained the largest economy at market exchange rates (roughly $15 trillion in 2011–12 in contemporary data), the issuer of the main reserve currency, and by far the largest military spender with the only global network of bases and alliances. In the period it:
- ended its combat role in Iraq (the last troops left in December 2011);
- raised troop levels in Afghanistan before beginning a drawdown;
- declared a "pivot" or "rebalance" toward Asia (2011–12).

**The European Union.** Its combined output was comparable to that of the United States, but in 2010–12 its agenda was taken over by the euro area's survival. Germany's fiscal weight and the European Central Bank's balance sheet became the decisive levers, and France, Italy and Spain negotiated against them.

**Other major powers.**
- **Japan** was the third-largest economy, a large creditor and a technology leader, but was politically unsettled and absorbing the 2011 disaster.
- **Russia** was a nuclear power and major hydrocarbon exporter. It was rebuilding influence in the former Soviet space through a customs union with Belarus and Kazakhstan, launched in 2010, and it joined the WTO in August 2012.
- **India** was a large, fast-growing but slowing economy, run by a coalition beset by corruption scandals.
- **Brazil** was a regional leader and commodity power.

**Regional powers and blocs.**
- In the Middle East, Saudi Arabia, Iran, Turkey, Qatar and Israel were the main poles, now competing over the outcomes of the uprisings.
- In Africa, South Africa, Nigeria, Ethiopia and Kenya carried regional weight. The African Union and ECOWAS mediated in Côte d'Ivoire (2010–11), Mali (2012) and elsewhere.
- In Southeast Asia, ASEAN tried to manage relations with China. In July 2012 its foreign ministers failed for the first time to issue a joint communiqué because they could not agree on the South China Sea.
- The "BRICS" label became an institution. South Africa joined in 2010–11, and summits were held in Brasília (2010), Sanya (2011) and New Delhi (2012).

**Non-state and quasi-state actors** also structured power:
- Al-Qaeda affiliates in Yemen (AQAP), the Sahara (AQIM) and Somalia (al-Shabaab, which formally joined al-Qaeda in 2012).
- Boko Haram in northern Nigeria.
- Mexico's trafficking organisations.
- Syria's armed opposition factions.
- The Taliban.
- Hezbollah.
- State-capitalist firms such as China's state oil companies, Gazprom, Saudi Aramco and the sovereign wealth funds of the Gulf, Norway and Singapore.

### Economy and exchange

**Growth by region.** IMF vintages from the period give this picture. In April 2011 the Fund projected world growth of about 4.5% for 2011 and 2012. By October 2012 it had cut 2012 to 3.3%, with advanced economies at 1.3% (after 1.6% in 2011 and 3.0% in 2010), noting "policy tightening in response to capacity constraints" in emerging economies. These are contemporary vintages. Later revisions and the 2011 International Comparison Program round (which changed PPP-based estimates of the relative size of China and India) altered the numbers, so any single "growth rate" should be read as data that was revised later.

**Trade flows.** The main flows ran:
- manufactured goods from East Asia (China, South Korea, Taiwan, Japan and Southeast Asia) to the rest of the world;
- oil and gas from the Gulf, Russia, West Africa and Latin America to Asia, Europe and North America;
- iron ore, coal and soybeans from Australia, Brazil, Indonesia and Argentina to China.

China became the largest trading partner of Japan, South Korea, Australia, Brazil and South Africa, among others. Japan's exports to mainland China were 19.4% of its total in 2010, against about 15.4% to the United States (Japanese data reported by Reuters). The Strait of Hormuz, the Strait of Malacca, the Suez Canal and the Gulf of Aden, where Somali piracy peaked in 2010–11, were the key chokepoints.

**Disruptions.**
- **Euro area.** Greece received a first bailout in May 2010, followed by programmes for Ireland (late 2010) and Portugal (2011). The Private Sector Involvement exchange of March 2012 then imposed a 53.5% face-value haircut on bondholders, about €106 billion of debt relief, which IMF documents describe as the largest in modern history. Spain sought European support for its banks in 2012. In July 2012 ECB President Mario Draghi pledged to do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro, and in September 2012 the ECB announced a conditional bond-buying facility, Outright Monetary Transactions. At the end of 2012 it had not yet been used.
- **United States.** A debt-ceiling standoff in 2011 led to a first-ever downgrade of US sovereign debt by Standard & Poor's. At the end of 2012 the "fiscal cliff" of automatic tax rises and spending cuts was still unresolved.
- **Currency tensions.** Brazil's finance minister Guido Mantega spoke of a "currency war" in 2010. Emerging economies complained that quantitative easing in the United States, Britain and Japan was pushing capital and currency appreciation onto them.
- **Food.** The FAO Food Price Index set a record in January 2011 (231 points) and peaked at about 236–238 points in February 2011. Its 2011 average of 228 points was the highest since the index began in 1990. Drivers included Russia's 2010 drought and export ban, energy prices, biofuel demand and a weak dollar. The 2012 US Midwest drought pushed maize and soybean prices up again.

### Social and demographic conditions

**Urbanisation.** China's 2010 census communiqué (National Bureau of Statistics, April 2011) recorded 665,575,306 urban residents, 49.68% of the population, and 261,386,075 people living away from their place of household registration for more than six months. India's census showed slower decadal growth (about 17.7% from 2001 to 2011, the first time since 1911–21 that the absolute addition fell) and a worsening child sex ratio (914 girls per 1,000 boys).

**Youth and livelihoods.** Youth unemployment was a shared grievance:
- In Arab states, high youth unemployment combined with rising food prices and anger at police abuse and corruption.
- In southern Europe, youth unemployment rose above 50% in Spain and Greece by 2012 in Eurostat data.
- In sub-Saharan Africa, rapid population growth met expanding but uneven growth in commodity-driven economies.

**Displacement.**
- Syria's war produced refugee flows into Jordan, Lebanon, Turkey and Iraq. In December 2012 UNHCR reported 509,559 Syrians registered or awaiting registration (Lebanon 154,387; Jordan 142,664; Turkey 136,319; Iraq 64,449), and its US$1 billion Regional Response Plan assumed up to 1 million refugees would need help in the first half of 2013.
- The Somali famine drove movement to the Dadaab (Kenya) and Dollo Ado (Ethiopia) camps.
- Mali's northern conflict displaced, by UN estimates, hundreds of thousands in 2012.
- In Libya in 2011, hundreds of thousands of migrant workers, many from sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, fled.
- In South Sudan, which became independent on 9 July 2011, returnees and border fighting with Sudan (the Heglig clashes, April 2012) caused further displacement.

**Religion and public life.**
- Islamist parties won the first post-uprising elections: Ennahda in Tunisia (October 2011), the Freedom and Justice Party and the Salafist Nour Party in Egypt, and the Justice and Development Party in Morocco.
- The role of sharia, of religious minorities (Copts in Egypt, Christians in northern Nigeria, Alawites and Christians in Syria) and of Sunni–Shia competition (Bahrain, Syria, Iraq, Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province) became central political questions.
- In China, the Party promoted "socialist core values" and a 2011 decision on "cultural system reform".
- In India, debates over caste, corruption and communal politics shaped national politics.

### Environment and natural events

- **Haiti, 12 January 2010.** The magnitude-7.0 earthquake's death toll is contested. The Haitian government's figure rose from about 222,570 (March 2010) to 316,000 (announced January 2011). A draft study commissioned by USAID (May 2011) put the toll at 46,000–85,000, and an academic survey by Kolbe and Muggah in the journal *Medicine, Conflict and Survival* gave about 158,000. A cholera epidemic followed from October 2010.
- **Russia, summer 2010.** Heat, wildfires and drought led Moscow to ban grain exports, feeding the global food-price rise.
- **Pakistan, July–August 2010.** Monsoon floods on the Indus submerged about a fifth of the country. Pakistani official figures (National Disaster Management Authority) record 1,985 deaths and about 20 million people affected, and the government put total economic losses at up to $43 billion. The 2011 floods in Sindh hit a population that had not yet recovered.
- **Japan, 11 March 2011.** The magnitude-9.0 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami killed 15,854 people by the National Police Agency's March 2012 tally, with 3,155 still missing. The tsunami caused meltdowns at Tokyo Electric Power's Fukushima Daiichi plant and forced evacuations. This led to a national debate over nuclear power, and most of Japan's reactors were offline by 2012. Germany decided in 2011 to phase out nuclear power, and China paused approvals of new plants for a time.
- **Horn of Africa, 2010–11.** The driest year in about 60 years, combined with high food prices and limits on aid access in areas held by al-Shabaab, led the UN to declare famine in parts of southern Somalia in July 2011. The declaration was lifted in February 2012. Contemporary warning systems (FSNAU and FEWS NET) had issued alerts from August 2010.
- **Other events.** Thailand's 2011 floods disrupted global electronics and car supply chains. The Sahel faced a food crisis in 2012. North America had the 2012 drought and Hurricane Sandy (October 2012). The Kyoto Protocol's first commitment period expired in 2012, and climate talks (Cancún 2010, Durban 2011, Doha 2012) kept negotiating toward a new agreement.

### Flashpoints of the period (as they looked at the end of 2012)

1. **Syria and its neighbours.** A conflict involving the government of Bashar al-Assad, many armed opposition groups (including jihadist factions such as Jabhat al-Nusra), and outside patrons: Iran and Hezbollah on one side; Turkey, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and Western states on the other; Russia and China blocking Security Council action. It had cross-border spillover risk into Lebanon, Turkey, Iraq and Jordan, and open questions over Syria's chemical weapons stockpile.
2. **Iran's nuclear programme.** International sanctions tightened (US financial measures, and the EU oil embargo in effect from July 2012), accompanied by open Israeli debate about military strikes and assassinations of Iranian nuclear scientists. Talks with the "P5+1" in Istanbul, Baghdad and Moscow in 2012 produced no agreement.
3. **Maritime Asia.** China and the Philippines had a standoff at Scarborough Shoal (April–June 2012). Japan's government bought three of the Senkaku (Diaoyu) islands on 10 September 2012 from a private owner, to prevent a purchase by Tokyo governor Shintaro Ishihara. Beijing responded by declaring territorial-sea baselines around the islands, sending maritime law-enforcement ships, and allowing sometimes violent anti-Japanese demonstrations. Earlier, in 2010, North Korea sank the South Korean corvette *Cheonan* (by the findings of a South Korean-led investigation, which Pyongyang denied) and shelled Yeonpyeong Island. Kim Jong-il died in December 2011, and in December 2012 his successor Kim Jong-un's regime launched a satellite on a long-range rocket.
4. **The Sahel and northern Nigeria.** Tuareg rebels (MNLA), some returning armed from Libya, launched an offensive in January 2012. The coup in Bamako on 22 March 2012 was followed by the loss of Kidal, Gao and Timbuktu, and by the MNLA's declaration of an independent "Azawad" on 6 April. Islamist groups (Ansar Dine, MUJAO, AQIM) then pushed the MNLA out of the towns by mid-2012. In late 2012 ECOWAS and the African Union were planning an African-led intervention, authorised by UN Security Council Resolution 2085 (December 2012). In Nigeria, Boko Haram's attacks escalated.
5. **The euro area's political economy.** Although it was not a military flashpoint, a disorderly Greek exit, or a loss of market access by Spain or Italy, was widely seen in 2011–12 as able to cause a global financial shock.

### Emerging forces

- **Mobile and social-media communication.** Its role in mobilisation (Tunisia, Egypt, Russia, Occupy) was widely debated. Commentators disagreed about whether it was a cause or a channel.
- **Mobile money and biometric identity** in the global South (M-Pesa, Aadhaar).
- **Unconventional oil and gas in North America.** Its effect on world energy balances was still uncertain.
- **Chinese outward lending and investment** in Africa, Latin America and Central Asia, alongside the renminbi's gradual internationalisation (offshore trading in Hong Kong, bilateral currency swaps).
- **New multilateral formats** led by emerging economies: BRICS summits discussing a joint development bank (proposed at New Delhi in 2012), and the G20 as the main economic forum.
- **Political Islam in government** through elections in Tunisia, Egypt and Morocco. Whether it would govern pluralistically or not was genuinely open.
- **Cheap solar panels.** Chinese manufacturing drove panel prices sharply down, leading to US and EU trade cases in 2012.
- **Drones.** US strikes in Pakistan, Yemen and Somalia made them a new form of armed force.

### Systemic fragilities

- **Euro area.** A self-reinforcing loop between sovereigns and banks, in a currency union without a joint fiscal authority or a banking union. The June 2012 summit agreed in principle to move toward one.
- **Food.** Dependence on world grain markets in import-reliant regions (the Middle East, North Africa, the Sahel, the Horn of Africa), with little buffer against harvest shocks in a few exporters.
- **Energy.** Exposure to chokepoints (Hormuz amid Iran tensions) and to ageing or overloaded grids. India's 30–31 July 2012 grid failures cut power to more than 600 million people across about 20 states, after a weak monsoon that reduced hydropower and coal shortages.
- **China.** A credit and property boom created by the 2009 stimulus, with local-government financing vehicles whose scale was uncertain.
- **Successions.** Tightly held successions in authoritarian systems (Saudi Arabia's ageing leadership, North Korea's untested young leader, Algeria, Zimbabwe), and questions about how far regimes that had withstood 2011 could be relied on.
- **Disaster exposure.** Very large, dense coastal and river-basin populations (the Ganges–Brahmaputra, Indus and Mekong basins, the Pearl River Delta, Japan's Pacific coast).

## III. Major Powers and Regional Blocs

### China (People's Republic)

**Internal politics.** The Hu Jintao–Wen Jiabao leadership managed a planned ten-year transfer of power. The Bo Xilai affair broke that plan's smoothness:
- In February 2012, Chongqing police chief Wang Lijun fled to the US consulate in Chengdu.
- Bo's wife, Gu Kailai, was later convicted of murdering British businessman Neil Heywood.
- Bo was removed as Chongqing party chief in March 2012, suspended from the Politburo in April, and expelled from the Party in September 2012 (ratified by the Central Committee's plenum on 4 November), facing criminal prosecution.

The 18th Congress (8–14 November 2012) made Xi Jinping General Secretary and Chairman of the Central Military Commission. Li Keqiang was placed to become Premier in March 2013, and the Standing Committee was reduced to seven members. Contemporary analysts disagreed about the balance between the networks of Jiang Zemin and Hu Jintao in the new line-up, and about whether Xi would bring political or only economic reform. Unrest was recorded throughout the period:
- The Wukan village land protest (late 2011), resolved by negotiation.
- Continued Tibetan self-immolations (2011–12).
- Tension in Xinjiang.
- Labour disputes, including the Honda strikes of 2010.

**External relations.** China:
- held assertive positions in the South and East China Seas;
- deepened trade ties with nearly every region;
- vetoed, with Russia, Security Council resolutions on Syria (October 2011, February 2012, July 2012);
- cut exports of rare earth elements after the September 2010 Senkaku collision incident, according to Japanese reports that Beijing denied.

**Economic basis.**
- World-leading manufacturing, construction and exports.
- Official growth above 10% in 2010, slowing toward about 7–8% in 2012 (National Bureau of Statistics). Some economists questioned the reliability of provincial and national figures.
- High investment and a rising problem of local-government debt.
- The 12th Five-Year Plan (2011–15) aimed to "rebalance" toward consumption.

**Knowledge and technique.**
- Tiangong-1, launched 2011, and the first crewed docking with Shenzhou-9, which carried China's first woman astronaut, Liu Yang (June 2012).
- A rapidly expanding high-speed-rail network, shaken by the Wenzhou collision of July 2011 and the dismissal of Railway Minister Liu Zhijun.
- The Tianhe-1A supercomputer, ranked the world's fastest in November 2010.
- Growing output of scientific publications.
- Mo Yan's 2012 Nobel Prize in Literature.

**Key individuals beyond the principal ruler.** Wen Jiabao, Wang Qishan, Zhou Yongkang (security chief until 2012), Bo Xilai, Li Keqiang, People's Bank governor Zhou Xiaochuan, the activist artist Ai Weiwei (detained in 2011), and the imprisoned 2010 Nobel Peace laureate Liu Xiaobo.

**Key organisations.** The CCP Politburo and the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, the People's Liberation Army, the state-owned "national champion" companies (CNPC/PetroChina, Sinopec, State Grid, China Development Bank), Huawei, and the rising internet firms Tencent, Alibaba and Baidu. Sina Weibo, the microblogging platform, became a space of public debate that was policed but had real influence.

### United States

**Internal politics.**
- Health reform passed (March 2010).
- The Dodd–Frank financial regulation law passed (July 2010).
- Republicans, energised by the Tea Party movement, won the House of Representatives (November 2010).
- The 2011 debt-ceiling crisis and credit downgrade.
- Occupy Wall Street (from September 2011).
- Obama re-elected over Mitt Romney (November 2012).

**External relations.**
- Withdrawal from Iraq (December 2011).
- A surge and then a planned drawdown in Afghanistan.
- The killing of Osama bin Laden at Abbottabad, Pakistan (May 2011), which badly damaged relations with Pakistan.
- Participation in the 2011 Libya air campaign.
- Sanctions against Iran.
- The Asia "rebalance".
- The attack on the US diplomatic compound in Benghazi (September 2012), which killed Ambassador Christopher Stevens.

**Economic basis.** Services, technology and finance, with a recovery driven by the Federal Reserve's quantitative easing (QE2 in 2010, QE3 in September 2012) and a rise in domestic energy output.

**Knowledge and technique.** The Mars rover Curiosity landed in August 2012. Smartphone and cloud platforms (Apple, Google, Facebook, Amazon) spread worldwide. Shale production grew.

**Key individuals.** Ben Bernanke, Hillary Clinton (Secretary of State), Timothy Geithner, David Petraeus, and technology figures such as Steve Jobs (died 2011) and Mark Zuckerberg.

**Key organisations.** The Federal Reserve, the Pentagon and the CIA, Apple and Google, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan, and the WikiLeaks disclosures of US diplomatic cables (2010) as a non-state challenge to secrecy.

### The European Union and the euro area

**Internal politics.**
- Governments fell or changed under pressure of the crisis: Greece (George Papandreou replaced by the technocrat Lucas Papademos in November 2011, then two elections in 2012 and a coalition under Antonis Samaras); Italy (Silvio Berlusconi replaced by Mario Monti, November 2011); Spain (Mariano Rajoy's Popular Party won, November 2011); France (François Hollande elected, May 2012).
- The Fiscal Compact was signed in March 2012. The UK stayed out.
- Anti-austerity protest was strong in Greece and Spain.

**External relations.**
- France and Britain led the Libya intervention.
- The EU imposed an oil embargo on Iran.
- Relations with Turkey's EU membership bid stalled.
- The EU received the 2012 Nobel Peace Prize.

**Economic basis.** An industrial core in Germany, the Netherlands and northern Italy against current-account deficit states in the south. The euro area was in recession in 2012 by IMF projections.

**Key individuals.** Angela Merkel, Wolfgang Schäuble, Nicolas Sarkozy, Jean-Claude Trichet and then Mario Draghi at the ECB, José Manuel Barroso, Herman Van Rompuy, and Jean-Claude Juncker (Eurogroup chairman).

**Key organisations.** The ECB, the "troika" (European Commission, ECB and IMF), the EFSF and ESM (the ESM launched in October 2012), Germany's Constitutional Court (which ruled on the ESM in September 2012), and Greece's SYRIZA and Golden Dawn parties, which rose in the 2012 elections.

### India

**Internal politics.** Manmohan Singh's second United Progressive Alliance government, led by the Congress party (Sonia Gandhi as party president), was weakened by scandals:
- the 2G telecom licence scandal;
- the 2010 Commonwealth Games contracts;
- coal-block allocations (2012).

Anna Hazare's 2011 fasts for a Lokpal (anti-corruption ombudsman) law drew mass support. Arvind Kejriwal founded the Aam Aadmi Party in November 2012. Pranab Mukherjee became President in July 2012. Narendra Modi won a third term as Gujarat chief minister in December 2012. The Maoist (Naxalite) insurgency in central India was described by the Prime Minister as the gravest internal security threat.

**External relations.** A strategic partnership with the United States (after the civil nuclear deal), rivalry and border talks with China, and a cautious thaw with Pakistan in trade and visas after the 2008 Mumbai attacks. The surviving Mumbai attacker, Ajmal Kasab, was executed in November 2012.

**Economic basis.** Growth was strong in 2010–11 and slowed sharply in 2012, with high inflation and a weaker rupee. Services and IT (Tata Consultancy Services, Infosys) were the strengths. In September 2012 the government opened multi-brand retail to foreign investment, a contested reform.

**Knowledge and technique.** By 2012 the Aadhaar programme's authority, UIDAI, had generated 21 crore (210 million) numbers since the first was issued on 29 September 2010, as reported by Deccan Herald. ISRO continued launches and planned a Mars orbiter. No polio case was recorded after January 2011, and the WHO removed India from the list of polio-endemic countries in 2012.

**Key individuals.** Sonia and Rahul Gandhi, Pranab Mukherjee, P. Chidambaram, Narendra Modi, Mamata Banerjee (who ended 34 years of Left Front rule in West Bengal, 2011), Anna Hazare, and Reserve Bank governor D. Subbarao.

### Japan

**Politics.** The Democratic Party of Japan had three prime ministers: Yukio Hatoyama (until June 2010), Naoto Kan and Yoshihiko Noda. Noda passed a law to double the consumption tax (2012) and dissolved the lower house for a December 2012 election.

**The 2011 disaster.** It reshaped energy policy. TEPCO was effectively nationalised in 2012.

**External relations.**
- The Senkaku crises of 2010 and 2012 with China.
- The Dokdo/Takeshima dispute with South Korea (President Lee Myung-bak's island visit, August 2012).
- The US alliance, and the Okinawa base dispute that helped end Hatoyama's government.

**Economic basis.** Advanced manufacturing and large net foreign assets, but also deflation, a very strong yen (at post-war highs in 2011) and the heaviest gross public debt in the industrial world.

**Key individuals.** Kan, Noda, Ishihara, Bank of Japan governor Masaaki Shirakawa, and opposition leader Shinzō Abe (LDP president from September 2012).

### Russia

**Politics.** The Putin–Medvedev "tandem" ran the state. The announced swap of posts (September 2011) and the disputed Duma election of 4 December 2011 set off protests:
- On 10 December 2011 at Bolotnaya Square, police estimated 25,000 and organisers 60,000.
- On 24 December on Sakharov Avenue, police said 28,000 and organisers 120,000.
- Pro-government counter-rallies were also large.
- Putin won the March 2012 presidential election with 63.6% officially.
- A clash on 6 May 2012 was followed by arrests, tighter laws on protest and on NGOs receiving foreign funds, the Pussy Riot trial (August 2012), and an opposition Coordination Council.

**External relations.** The US–Russia "reset" (the New START treaty, 2010; WTO entry, 2012), opposition to Western policy on Libya and Syria, and the Customs Union and planned Eurasian integration. The US Magnitsky Act (December 2012) strained relations.

**Economic basis.** Oil and gas (Gazprom, Rosneft), metals and grain, with budgets tied to oil prices.

**Key individuals.** Dmitry Medvedev, Alexei Kudrin (who resigned as finance minister in 2011), Patriarch Kirill, and opposition figures Alexei Navalny, Boris Nemtsov and Sergei Udaltsov.

### Brazil and Latin America

**Brazil.** Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's successor Dilma Rousseff took office in January 2011. She dismissed several ministers over corruption allegations, and the Supreme Court's *mensalão* trial (2012) convicted senior Workers' Party figures. Growth was 7.5% in 2010, then slowed sharply: the statistics office IBGE's early estimates were 2.7% for 2011 and about 1% for 2012 (the 2012 figure was later published as 0.9%, and the 2011 figure was later revised to 3.9%). Brazil was host-designate of the 2014 World Cup and the 2016 Olympics. Petrobras was developing deep-water "pre-salt" oil.

**The rest of the region.**
- **Mexico.** The PRI returned under Enrique Peña Nieto (elected July 2012). The toll of organised-crime violence under Felipe Calderón (2006–12) is contested. The government's own database counted 34,162 organised-crime-related killings from December 2006 to December 2010. Media commonly used about 50,000–60,000 for the whole term. Researcher Molly Molloy, using all homicides recorded by INEGI and the national public-security system (SNSP), projected more than 100,000 total homicides for the term.
- **Venezuela.** Hugo Chávez was re-elected in October 2012 and then left for cancer treatment in Cuba in December 2012, leaving the succession uncertain. He named Nicolás Maduro as his preferred successor.
- **Colombia.** Juan Manuel Santos opened peace talks with the FARC (Oslo, October 2012; Havana).
- **Argentina.** Cristina Fernández de Kirchner was re-elected (2011) and nationalised YPF (2012).
- **Chile.** Student protests (2011).
- **Cuba.** Raúl Castro's economic reforms (the 2011 Party Congress).
- **Paraguay.** President Fernando Lugo was impeached (June 2012), and Mercosur suspended Paraguay and admitted Venezuela.
- **Haiti.** Reconstruction was slow, and the country was dependent on aid and on UN peacekeeping (MINUSTAH).

### The Arab world, Iran and Turkey

- **Egypt.** After Mubarak fell, the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces under Field Marshal Mohamed Hussein Tantawi ruled. The Muslim Brotherhood and Salafists won parliamentary elections in 2011–12, but the Supreme Constitutional Court dissolved the parliament in June 2012. Mohamed Morsi won the June 2012 runoff with 51.73% (13,230,131 votes to 12,347,380 for Ahmed Shafiq; turnout 51.8%). He retired Tantawi in August 2012. His 22 November 2012 constitutional declaration made his decrees "final and binding and cannot be appealed" until a new constitution and parliament were in place. It triggered mass protests and deadly clashes outside the presidential palace, and he rescinded most of it on 8 December. The constitution was approved in a December 2012 referendum by 63.8% on a turnout of 32.9%. At the end of 2012 the country was deeply divided between Islamists and a broad opposition (the National Salvation Front), with the army's intentions unclear.
- **Tunisia.** An Ennahda-led coalition governed with secular partners (President Moncef Marzouki). Estimates of deaths in the uprising have changed over time: the interim government said about 78 (January 2011); a UN human-rights mission counted 219, including 72 in prison riots (February 2011); and the national fact-finding commission reported 338 dead and 2,147 injured (May 2012).
- **Libya.** The National Transitional Council was followed by the elected General National Congress (July 2012). Militias kept their power. The death toll of the 2011 war is highly contested: the NTC's health minister said 30,000 (September 2011) and later revised this to 25,000, while later official and academic counts are much lower.
- **Syria.** See Flashpoints. The UN human rights office's analysis of seven casualty lists produced 59,648 named dead between 15 March 2011 and 30 November 2012. It was published on 2 January 2013 and described by the office as probably a minimum. Opposition activist counts at the end of 2012 were about 45,000.
- **Yemen, Bahrain and the Gulf.** Yemen had the GCC-brokered transition, a National Dialogue in preparation, and continued AQAP activity. In Bahrain, protests were suppressed with the help of Saudi-led Peninsula Shield forces (March 2011), and the Bahrain Independent Commission of Inquiry reported in November 2011. Qatar, through Al Jazeera and financial and military support, and Saudi Arabia, through financial support to monarchies and conservative actors, both expanded their regional roles.
- **Iran.** Mahmoud Ahmadinejad was in conflict with Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei's circle. Sanctions and the EU embargo cut oil exports, and the rial fell sharply in 2012, with currency protests in Tehran in October. Iran backed Assad.
- **Turkey.** Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's AKP won a third term (June 2011) with about half the vote. Turkey had a model-of-reform image in the Arab world, broke with Damascus, hosted Syrian refugees and opposition bodies, and was in conflict with the PKK. It opened talks with Abdullah Öcalan in late 2012, as reported in Turkish media.
- **Iraq.** After the US withdrawal, Nouri al-Maliki's government faced Sunni resentment (the arrest warrant for Vice-President Tariq al-Hashimi, December 2011) and disputes with the Kurdistan Regional Government over oil.

### Sub-Saharan Africa

- **Nigeria.** Goodluck Jonathan won the April 2011 election. Post-election violence in the north killed about 800 people, by Human Rights Watch's count. Nationwide strikes followed the removal of the fuel subsidy in January 2012. Human Rights Watch's October 2012 report counted at least 550 deaths in about 115 Boko Haram attacks in 2011, and more than 815 in about 275 attacks in the first nine months of 2012. It also documented abuses by security forces that it said might amount to crimes against humanity.
- **Ethiopia.** Meles Zenawi died in August 2012 and Hailemariam Desalegn succeeded him in an orderly handover. The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile was announced in 2011, raising tension with Egypt.
- **The Sudans.** South Sudan became independent in July 2011. Oil output stopped (January 2012) in a dispute over transit fees, and a September 2012 cooperation agreement was signed in Addis Ababa.
- **Somalia.** AMISOM (Ugandan and Burundian troops, with Kenyan forces from late 2011) pushed al-Shabaab out of Mogadishu (August 2011) and Kismayo (September 2012). A new parliament elected Hassan Sheikh Mohamud president in September 2012.
- **Côte d'Ivoire.** Laurent Gbagbo refused to accept his loss in the 2010 election, which led to war. He was captured in April 2011 and sent to the International Criminal Court.
- **Eastern Congo.** The M23 rebellion took Goma in November 2012, and UN experts accused Rwanda of supporting the rebels, which Kigali denied.
- **South Africa.** Police killed 34 striking miners at Marikana in August 2012. The ANC's Mangaung conference (December 2012) re-elected Jacob Zuma and elected Cyril Ramaphosa deputy.
- **Economy.** Many African economies were among the world's fastest growing on commodity exports and Chinese trade.

### Southeast Asia, South Asia's other states, and the Koreas

- **Myanmar.** The military-backed government of Thein Sein began liberalising from 2011: Aung San Suu Kyi was released (November 2010) and won a by-election (April 2012), and Western sanctions were eased. Communal violence broke out in Rakhine State in 2012 against the Rohingya.
- **Indonesia.** Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono presided over growth of 6.5% in 2011 and 6.23% in 2012, according to BPS-Statistics Indonesia.
- **Thailand.** After the 2010 Red Shirt crackdown in Bangkok, in which about 90 people died, Yingluck Shinawatra won the 2011 election.
- **The Philippines.** Benigno Aquino III took office (2010), signed a framework agreement with the Moro Islamic Liberation Front (October 2012), and confronted China at sea.
- **Pakistan.** The Supreme Court removed Prime Minister Yousuf Raza Gilani (June 2012). Relations with the US were in crisis after Abbottabad and the Salala border-post strike (November 2011). Militant violence continued, including the attack on Malala Yousafzai (October 2012).
- **Afghanistan.** The Karzai government negotiated the transition of security to Afghan forces by 2014.
- **The Koreas.** South Korea (Samsung and Hyundai as global leaders) elected Park Geun-hye in December 2012. North Korea's leadership passed to Kim Jong-un.

## IV. Thematic Sections

### Knowledge, science, and technology

**Physics and space.**
- CERN's ATLAS and CMS experiments announced a new boson consistent with the Higgs on 4 July 2012.
- The US rover Curiosity landed on Mars.
- China carried out its first crewed orbital docking.
- In 2012 the Daya Bay experiment in China measured neutrino oscillation parameters.

**Computing and communications.**
- Smartphones (the iPhone and Android devices, with growing Chinese brands such as Huawei and ZTE) and tablets spread widely.
- Social platforms (Facebook, Twitter, Weibo, WeChat launched 2011) grew.
- Cloud computing was adopted.
- Japan's K computer led the supercomputer rankings in 2011.

**Life sciences.** Genome sequencing costs fell. Induced pluripotent stem cells won Shinya Yamanaka a share of the 2012 Nobel Prize in Medicine. HIV antiretroviral treatment was scaled up across Africa.

**Energy.** Shale drilling expanded, solar-panel costs fell, and nuclear power was reassessed after Fukushima.

**Attribution.** Contributions came heavily from East Asia (China, Japan, South Korea), India's frugal-engineering and IT-services model, Israel's technology sector, and Brazil's agricultural research (Embrapa).

### Health and population

**Disease.**
- The Haiti cholera epidemic, from October 2010.
- Polio persisted in Pakistan, Afghanistan and Nigeria. In Pakistan, attacks on vaccinators began in December 2012.
- HIV prevalence stabilised in many settings as treatment expanded.
- Malaria deaths fell in WHO estimates, though the figures are model-based.
- A new coronavirus was identified in Saudi Arabia in September 2012, but its importance was not yet known.

**Fukushima.** It raised fears about radiation exposure. Official assessments at the time reported no deaths directly from radiation, while evacuation-related deaths among the elderly were already being recorded.

**Famine and hunger.** Famine mortality in Somalia was grave but could not be precisely counted at the time. A later study for FAO's FSNAU and FEWS NET (published May 2013) estimated about 258,000 excess deaths between October 2010 and April 2012, within a plausible range of about 244,000–273,000 and a wider bound of 143,000–273,000. About half were children under five.

**Demographic pressures.** Ageing in Japan, Europe and, increasingly, China. A youth bulge in the Arab world, sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Skewed sex ratios in China and India.

### Security and conflict

**Major armed conflicts.**
- Afghanistan–Pakistan.
- Syria.
- Libya (2011).
- Yemen.
- Somalia.
- Mali.
- Northern Nigeria.
- Eastern Congo.
- The Sudan–South Sudan border and Darfur, Southern Kordofan and Blue Nile.
- Mexico's criminal wars.
- Iraq's continuing insurgency.
- Colombia.
- Myanmar's ethnic borderlands (the Kachin conflict resumed in 2011).
- India's Maoist belt.
- The Israel–Gaza war of November 2012 ("Pillar of Defence"), ended by an Egyptian-brokered ceasefire.

**Diplomacy.** The Security Council authorised force in Libya (Resolution 1973, March 2011). Russia and China's subsequent view that the Libya mandate had been abused shaped their vetoes on Syria. The Palestinian Authority won UN non-member observer state status (November 2012). The ICC's first verdict convicted Thomas Lubanga (March 2012).

**Techniques and organisation.**
- Armed drones.
- Cyber operations: the Stuxnet worm against Iran's centrifuges became public in 2010, and a cyber-attack struck Saudi Aramco in 2012.
- Special-forces raids.
- China's first aircraft carrier, Liaoning (commissioned September 2012), and its anti-access capabilities.
- Maritime law-enforcement agencies used as instruments of territorial claims.
- Proxy support to non-state fighters in Syria and Libya.
- Returning fighters and weapons from Libya spreading into the Sahel.

### Culture and society

**Protest culture.** A widely shared repertoire of occupying squares (Tahrir, Puerta del Sol, Zuccotti Park, Bolotnaya), online mobilisation and slogans against corruption and for "dignity" (*karama*). In the Arab world, protest poetry, street art and satire flourished (the Egyptian satirist Bassem Youssef's programme moved to television).

**Religion and politics.** Religious authorities took on new political roles: Al-Azhar's documents on freedoms (2011–12), the Salafist entry into electoral politics, and the Russian Orthodox Church's alignment with the state. In December 2012 Pope Benedict XVI joined Twitter. The "Innocence of Muslims" video provoked protests across the Muslim world in September 2012.

**Popular culture.** Culture flowed along new routes:
- K-pop's global reach (Psy's "Gangnam Style" became the most-viewed YouTube video in 2012).
- Nollywood.
- Bollywood.
- Turkish television dramas sold across the Middle East and the Balkans.
- Chinese online literature.
- The Brazilian telenovela.

**Social norms.** Several norms were contested:
- Same-sex marriage spread in some Western and Latin American jurisdictions (Argentina 2010).
- Gender violence was debated after the December 2012 Delhi gang-rape, which set off nationwide protests in India.
- Women's roles in post-uprising constitutions were argued over.

## V. Conclusion

In 2010–12 the world was moving from a US-centred order toward a more distributed one without yet having arrived. Asia held most of humanity, and a growing share of manufacturing and savings were Asian. China's leadership handover in November 2012 made the direction of the world's second-largest economy one of the central open questions. The Atlantic economies still held the reserve currency, the deepest financial markets and the greatest military reach, but they were absorbed in their own debts. The euro's survival appeared more secure after the ECB's pledges of mid-2012, though this was untested.

The live tensions at the end of 2012 were these:
- whether Egypt's and Tunisia's elected Islamist-led governments and their opponents could agree on constitutional rules;
- whether Syria's war would end by negotiation, by victory for one side or by partition, and how far it would spread into neighbouring states;
- whether sanctions and diplomacy would settle the Iranian nuclear question or lead to force;
- whether maritime disputes in the East and South China Seas would stay at the level of coast-guard confrontations;
- whether an African-led force could restore Bamako's control of northern Mali;
- whether China could rebalance its growth;
- whether India would regain momentum before its 2014 election;
- whether the commodity boom supporting Africa, Latin America, Russia and the Gulf would last.

Each of these was a genuinely open question at the time.
