# Ground-Truth World-State Report: 2014 to 2016

## I. Executive Summary

Seen from end-2016, the three years from 2014 to 2016 were a period in which the post-2008 global economy never fully regained momentum, in which the centre of gravity of global production continued to shift toward Asia, and in which a string of shocks — an oil price collapse, a Chinese financial scare, epidemics, wars and mass displacement — tested an international order that many contemporaries described as fraying.

**Major political shifts.** Across Asia, a new generation of strong executives consolidated power: Xi Jinping in China, deepening an anti-corruption campaign and centralising authority; Narendra Modi in India, elected in May 2014 with the first single-party parliamentary majority in three decades; Joko Widodo in Indonesia (2014); Rodrigo Duterte in the Philippines (2016). Myanmar held an election in November 2015 that brought Aung San Suu Kyi's National League for Democracy to government. In Africa, Nigeria's March 2015 election produced the first defeat of an incumbent president by an opposition candidate in that country's history, when Muhammadu Buhari beat Goodluck Jonathan and Jonathan conceded. In Latin America, the commodity-funded left-leaning governments of the 2000s came under pressure: Brazil's President Dilma Rousseff was impeached and removed in 2016 amid recession and the Lava Jato corruption probe; Argentina elected Mauricio Macri in late 2015; Venezuela slid into an economic and political emergency; and Colombia concluded a peace agreement with the FARC that voters narrowly rejected in October 2016 before a revised version was ratified by Congress. In the post-Soviet space, Ukraine's Maidan revolution was followed by Russia's annexation of Crimea (March 2014) and war in the Donbas. In the Middle East, the Islamic State group seized Mosul in June 2014 and declared a "caliphate," Russia intervened militarily in Syria in September 2015, a Saudi-led coalition entered Yemen's war in March 2015, Iran and six powers reached a nuclear agreement in July 2015, and Turkey survived an attempted coup in July 2016. In Europe and North America, the Greek debt crisis peaked in 2015, the UK voted in June 2016 to leave the European Union, and in November 2016 Donald Trump won the US presidency against the expectation of most forecasters.

**Economic changes.** Global growth hovered around 3% per year by IMF measures, with advanced economies slow and emerging economies diverging sharply. Oil prices fell by more than 70% between mid-2014 and early 2016, redistributing income from exporters to importers. China's leadership described a "new normal" of slower growth and rebalancing toward consumption and services, while a stock-market bubble burst in mid-2015 and an August 2015 change to the renminbi fixing unsettled global markets. Central banks in Europe and Japan pushed deeper into unconventional policy, including negative interest rates, while the US Federal Reserve began raising rates in December 2015. India launched a dramatic demonetisation in November 2016, withdrawing 86% of its currency by value from legal tender.

**Social developments.** Forced displacement reached the highest levels UNHCR had recorded. More than a million people crossed the Mediterranean to Europe in 2015, and the political reaction reshaped European politics. Urbanisation, the spread of smartphones and mobile payments across China, India and Africa, and the rise of social media as a political arena changed how billions of people communicated, organised and transacted.

**Advances in knowledge and technique.** The period saw the first direct detection of gravitational waves (announced February 2016), a machine-learning system defeating a top human player at Go (March 2016), rapid diffusion of CRISPR gene editing and the first attempts to edit human embryos in China, China's launch of a quantum-communications satellite, India's Mars Orbiter Mission reaching Mars orbit (September 2014), and reusable-rocket landings in the United States.

**Crises and challenges.** The 2014–16 West African Ebola epidemic, the Zika virus emergency in the Americas, the Syrian, Iraqi, Yemeni, Libyan, South Sudanese, Ukrainian and Lake Chad basin conflicts, mass-casualty terrorism in cities from Paris and Brussels to Garissa, Ankara and Lahore, and a strong 2015–16 El Niño that drove drought in the Horn and Southern Africa and record global temperatures.

**Overall characterisation.** By end-2016 the world was richer, more connected and more urban than ever, but its growth engines were sputtering, its main institutions were contested, and power was spreading from a US-centred system toward a more crowded field in which China was the only plausible peer economy, Russia and regional powers were testing boundaries by force, and non-state armed movements controlled significant territory. Contemporaries debated whether they were witnessing "secular stagnation," the end of the post-Cold War order, or merely a difficult transition.

## II. Global Overview

### Distribution of power

In 2014–2016 the principal units of power were nation-states, supranational blocs (the EU, ASEAN, the African Union, the Eurasian Economic Union launched in 2015), alliances (NATO, the US hub-and-spoke system in Asia), new and old multilateral financial institutions, non-state armed movements holding territory, and transnational corporations.

- **The United States** remained the only power with global military reach and the issuer of the dominant reserve currency. The Obama administration pursued a "rebalance" to Asia, concluded the Trans-Pacific Partnership negotiations (signed February 2016), led the coalition air campaign against the Islamic State from August 2014, restored diplomatic ties with Cuba (announced December 2014), and co-led the Iran nuclear deal and the Paris climate agreement. At end-2016, the incoming administration's stated scepticism toward trade agreements and alliances left allies and rivals uncertain about US commitments.
- **China** was the world's largest manufacturer and trading nation in goods and the second-largest economy at market exchange rates. Under Xi Jinping it moved from "keeping a low profile" toward active institution-building: the Belt and Road Initiative (announced 2013 and elaborated through 2014–16), the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, the BRICS New Development Bank, and island-building in the Spratlys. The renminbi entered the IMF's Special Drawing Rights basket in October 2016.
- **Russia** asserted a sphere of influence by force: Crimea's annexation in March 2014, support for separatist forces in Donetsk and Luhansk, and from September 2015 a direct air campaign in Syria that shifted that war's balance toward the Assad government. Western sanctions and the oil collapse squeezed its economy, and it deepened its partnership with China.
- **The European Union** remained the largest single market but was absorbed by overlapping crises — the Greek debt emergency, the 2015–16 refugee influx, terrorism, the conflict with Russia over Ukraine, and the UK referendum result.
- **India** was the fastest-growing large economy by official figures, and the Modi government pursued an "Act East" policy, investment promotion ("Make in India") and closer US and Japanese ties while keeping its Russian defence relationship.
- **Japan** under Shinzo Abe pursued "Abenomics" and reinterpreted its constitution to allow collective self-defence (security laws passed in 2015).
- **Regional powers** with major agency included Saudi Arabia (under King Salman from January 2015, with Deputy Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman driving the Yemen intervention and the April 2016 "Vision 2030"), Iran (under President Hassan Rouhani, with the Revolutionary Guards' Quds Force extending influence in Iraq and Syria), Turkey (under President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan), Brazil, Nigeria, South Africa, Indonesia, Pakistan and Egypt.
- **Non-state armed actors** controlled real territory: the Islamic State at its peak held large areas of Iraq and Syria; Boko Haram held territory in north-east Nigeria in 2014–early 2015; the Houthi movement (Ansar Allah) took Sanaa in September 2014; al-Shabaab, al-Qaeda affiliates in Yemen and the Sahel, the Taliban in Afghanistan, and Kurdish forces (the Iraqi Kurdistan Regional Government and the Syrian YPG) were all significant actors.

Zones of tension clustered at the edges of the great powers' spheres: the Russia–NATO frontier, the East and South China Seas, the Korean peninsula, the Persian Gulf, the India–Pakistan line of control, and the Levant.

### Economy and exchange

**Growth.** The IMF's January 2016 WEO Update estimated global growth at 3.1% in 2015; its October 2016 WEO projected 3.1% for 2016 and 3.4% for 2017, marking down advanced-economy growth to 1.6% after the UK's referendum and a weak US first half. IMF chief economist Maurice Obstfeld summarised: "Taken as a whole, the world economy has moved sideways." The IMF warned that persistent stagnation could "fuel populist calls for restrictions on trade and immigration." Trade growth had slowed since 2012 relative to both historical performance and output growth — a concern widely discussed at the time as "peak trade" or the "global trade slowdown."

**Commodities.** Brent crude peaked at $115.06 per barrel on 19 June 2014 (US Energy Information Administration) and fell to a close of $26.01 in January 2016 — which the California Energy Commission's Petroleum Watch, citing EIA data, called "the lowest prices since September 2003." The causes were contested even then: the EIA pointed to weakening demand, rising US shale production, fewer supply disruptions and OPEC's decision in November 2014 to hold output; academic analyses divided between supply-side and demand-side explanations. Metals and agricultural prices also fell as Chinese demand for raw materials slowed. At its 30 November 2016 meeting OPEC agreed to cut production, with non-OPEC producers including Russia moving toward coordination — an arrangement contemporaries viewed as fragile.

**China's economy.** Official data from the National Bureau of Statistics reported 7.3% growth in 2014 and 6.9% in 2015, the slowest since 1990, with services growing 8.3% and reaching over half of GDP. Independent estimates at the time were lower: Oxford Economics estimated 6.3% for 2015, Rabobank's activity indicator suggested "closer to 5% than 7%," and Capital Economics put fourth-quarter 2015 growth near 4.5%; more extreme commentators suggested far lower figures. Proxies such as electricity output, rail freight and trade volumes stagnated or fell in 2015. The Shanghai Composite peaked at 5,166 on 12 June 2015 after a margin-fuelled rally, then fell roughly 43% by late August; the authorities responded with a state-backed buying "national team," trading suspensions and restrictions on large shareholders' sales. On 11 August 2015 the People's Bank of China changed the renminbi fixing mechanism, producing a depreciation of about 1.9% — the largest in two decades — which markets read as a signal of weakness and which contributed to the global sell-off of 24 August ("Black Monday"). Capital outflows and a drawdown of foreign-exchange reserves followed in late 2015 and 2016.

**Other major economies.** Brazil's economy contracted by 3.8% in 2015 according to IBGE, with a further severe contraction expected in 2016 — the deepest recession in its recorded history. Nigeria, Africa's largest economy, was forecast by the IMF (October 2016) to shrink by 1.7% in 2016 as oil revenue collapsed, and South Africa to barely grow. Russia's economy contracted in 2015 (Rosstat's preliminary estimate was −3.7%) after the ruble lost roughly 40–50% of its value against the dollar in 2014 and the Bank of Russia raised its key rate from 10.5% to 17% in a single overnight decision on 15–16 December 2014. Venezuela, dependent on oil for almost all export earnings, faced a collapse: its central bank stopped regular publication of economic data after January 2016, and the IMF's October 2016 projections, made without official figures, had GDP shrinking about 10% and inflation reaching about 475% in 2016. India reported the fastest growth of any large economy, though its revised GDP methodology (introduced in 2015) was debated among Indian economists. On 8 November 2016, Prime Minister Modi announced that ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes — 86% of currency in circulation by value — would cease to be legal tender at midnight, with the stated aims of curbing "black money," counterfeiting and terror finance; it caused severe cash shortages and a sharp domestic debate, with critics such as Jawaharlal Nehru University economist Jayati Ghosh calling it a "foolish mistake."

**Monetary regimes.** The European Central Bank launched large-scale bond purchases in January 2015; the Bank of Japan adopted negative rates in January 2016; the US Federal Reserve raised rates in December 2015 and again in December 2016. Low or negative yields across advanced economies were a defining financial condition of the period.

**Finance and trade architecture.** The AIIB opened in Beijing on 16 January 2016 with 57 founding members and authorised capital of $100 billion, despite US opposition; the UK's decision in March 2015 to seek membership was followed by other European states. The TPP was signed in February 2016 but its ratification was in doubt by end-2016; negotiations on the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, involving ASEAN, China, India and others, continued. China's Belt and Road framework and the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor were the flagship connective-infrastructure projects.

### Social and demographic conditions

The world's population was roughly 7.3–7.4 billion (UN Population Division, 2015 Revision). Asia held about three-fifths of humanity, and Africa was the fastest-growing region demographically. China announced in October 2015 the end of its one-child policy, permitting all couples two children from 2016, in response to ageing and a shrinking workforce.

Forced displacement reached record levels. UNHCR's Global Trends report counted 65.3 million people forcibly displaced at end-2015 (up from 59.5 million a year earlier): 21.3 million refugees, 40.8 million internally displaced people and 3.2 million asylum-seekers — one in every 113 people on earth. By end-2016 the figure was 65.6 million. The largest sources were Syria, Afghanistan and South Sudan, and the largest hosts were neighbouring developing countries — Turkey, Pakistan, Lebanon, Iran, Ethiopia, Jordan — with developing regions hosting 84% of refugees under UNHCR's mandate at end-2016. The crossing to Europe was the most visible part: UNHCR recorded 1,000,573 Mediterranean arrivals in 2015, with 3,735 dead or missing; IOM's count including land arrivals was about 1,005,504. Sea arrivals fell in 2016 to about 363,000 after the EU–Turkey statement of 18 March 2016, which provided that irregular migrants crossing to the Greek islands from 20 March would be returned to Turkey, but recorded deaths rose to over 5,000, making 2016 the deadliest year IOM had recorded in the Mediterranean.

Other social currents included the continued expansion of the Chinese and Indian middle classes, rapid adoption of smartphones and mobile payments, and youth unemployment and underemployment across the Middle East, North Africa, Southern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa. Religious life was marked by the Islamic State's sectarian violence and genocide against Yazidis (August 2014 onward), the persecution of Rohingya Muslims in Myanmar (with a military crackdown in northern Rakhine from October 2016), Pope Francis's emphasis on migrants, poverty and the environment (the 2015 encyclical Laudato si'), and the rising political weight of religious nationalism in several democracies.

### Environment and natural events

The 2015–16 El Niño was among the strongest on record. The WMO confirmed 2016 as the warmest year on record, about 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels and 0.06°C above the previous record set in 2015; Arctic sea ice fell to unprecedented lows for the season and early-2016 sea levels hit record highs. El Niño-linked drought struck Ethiopia (where the government and UN's 2016 Humanitarian Requirements Document, issued in December 2015, sought US$1.4 billion and emergency food assistance for 10.2 million people, compared with 4.5 million in the 2011 Horn of Africa crisis), Southern Africa, parts of India and Southeast Asia, and Central America; Indonesian peat fires in 2015 blanketed Southeast Asia in haze. Major disasters included the April 2015 Nepal earthquake, which killed over 8,790 people and injured 22,000 according to the Government of Nepal's Post Disaster Needs Assessment; Typhoon Haiyan's aftermath in the Philippines; the 2016 Ecuador earthquake; and Hurricane Matthew's destruction in Haiti in October 2016.

On climate policy, the Paris Agreement was adopted in December 2015, with parties committing to hold warming "well below 2°C" and pursue efforts toward 1.5°C, and it entered into force on 4 November 2016 after crossing the threshold of 55 parties representing 55% of emissions. The Sustainable Development Goals were adopted at the UN in September 2015.

### Flashpoints of the period (as judged at end-2016)

1. **The Syria–Iraq theatre.** The most intense multi-party war in the world, drawing in the US-led coalition, Russia, Iran and Hezbollah, Turkey, the Gulf states, Kurdish forces and jihadist movements. By December 2016 the Syrian government, backed by Russia and Iran, had retaken eastern Aleppo; the Iraqi-led offensive to retake Mosul from the Islamic State began in October 2016; Turkey had sent troops into northern Syria (August 2016). Risk of direct clashes among outside powers — highlighted when Turkey shot down a Russian jet in November 2015 — remained high.
2. **The Russia–Ukraine–NATO frontier.** The Minsk II agreement of February 2015 reduced but did not end fighting in the Donbas; NATO agreed in July 2016 to station rotational battalions in the Baltic states and Poland. The question of the incoming US administration's stance toward Russia was widely debated.
3. **The South and East China Seas and the Korean peninsula.** China's island-building and its rejection of the July 2016 arbitral award; the Philippines' pivot toward Beijing under Duterte; Japan–China friction around the Senkaku/Diaoyu islands; North Korea's two nuclear tests in 2016 and the South Korean decision to host the US THAAD missile-defence system, which China strongly opposed; and the December 2016 impeachment of South Korea's President Park Geun-hye.
4. **The Gulf–Yemen rivalry.** The Saudi–Iranian contest, sharpened by the January 2016 execution of Saudi Shia cleric Nimr al-Nimr and the severing of diplomatic ties, played out in Yemen, Syria, Bahrain and Lebanon.
5. **The India–Pakistan line of control.** Unrest in the Kashmir valley after the killing of militant commander Burhan Wani in July 2016, the Uri attack in September 2016 and India's announced cross-border "surgical strikes" raised fears of escalation between two nuclear-armed states.

Other live zones included the Lake Chad basin and Sahel, South Sudan (renewed fighting in Juba in July 2016), Libya's divided authority, Burundi's post-2015 crisis, and The Gambia, where President Yahya Jammeh reversed his concession after losing the December 2016 election.

### Emerging forces (judged as a contemporary might)

- **Chinese-led development finance and connectivity** — the AIIB, the New Development Bank, the Silk Road Fund and Belt and Road — which could either complement or compete with the Bretton Woods institutions.
- **Mobile platforms and digital payments** — Alipay and WeChat Pay in China, Kenya's M-Pesa, India's Aadhaar identity system and the Reliance Jio 4G launch (September 2016) — which were expanding financial access and state and corporate data-gathering at very large scale.
- **Machine learning ("deep learning")** — highlighted by DeepMind's AlphaGo victory over Lee Sedol in March 2016 — which contemporaries debated as either overhyped or a source of large labour-market disruption.
- **Gene editing (CRISPR)** — with a Chinese team's 2015 report of editing non-viable human embryos provoking an international summit on human gene editing in December 2015.
- **Cheap renewable energy and shale** — falling solar and wind costs and the resilience of US shale production, both of which were reshaping energy markets.
- **Blockchains and cryptocurrencies** — Bitcoin and the Ethereum platform (launched 2015), whose potential was debated; the 2016 hack of "The DAO" showed their fragility.
- **Anti-establishment and nationalist politics** across democracies, visible in the UK referendum, the US election, the rise of parties such as France's Front National, and populist leaders elsewhere; contemporaries debated whether this was a durable realignment.
- **Jihadist governance models** — the Islamic State's territorial "caliphate" and its franchise network — which, even as it lost territory in 2016, had inspired affiliates from Libya to Afghanistan and Southeast Asia.

### Systemic fragilities

- **Commodity dependence** of many state budgets (Gulf states, Russia, Nigeria, Angola, Venezuela, Algeria), exposed by the oil price collapse.
- **Chinese debt and capital-flight risk** — rapidly rising corporate debt, shadow banking and pressure on the renminbi.
- **Advanced-economy monetary exhaustion** — near-zero or negative rates leaving little room to respond to a new downturn.
- **Eurozone institutional incompleteness** — exposed in the Greek crisis and by Italian banking weakness (Italy's December 2016 constitutional referendum defeat prompted Prime Minister Matteo Renzi's resignation).
- **Epidemic preparedness gaps** — Ebola showed that weak health systems and a slow international response could allow a known pathogen to spread explosively.
- **El Niño and climate-related shocks** to rain-fed agriculture across Africa and South Asia.
- **Dependence on maritime chokepoints** — the Strait of Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb near the Yemen war zone, and the Strait of Malacca.
- **Legitimacy gaps and contested successions** — ageing leaders (e.g., Zimbabwe's Robert Mugabe, Algeria's Abdelaziz Bouteflika), term-limit disputes (Burundi, the DRC, where elections due in 2016 were postponed) and fragile transitions.

## III. Major Powers and Regional Blocs

### China

**Internal politics.** Xi Jinping, General Secretary since 2012, consolidated authority through an anti-corruption campaign led by Wang Qishan's Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, which brought down senior figures including former security chief Zhou Yongkang (sentenced 2015). In October 2016 the Party designated Xi its "core." Policy combined economic reform rhetoric (supply-side structural reform, cutting overcapacity in steel and coal) with tighter control of civil society, the internet and religion, including restrictions in Xinjiang and a crackdown on human-rights lawyers in July 2015. Hong Kong's 2014 "Umbrella Movement" contested Beijing's electoral framework. The 13th Five-Year Plan (2016–2020) targeted growth of at least 6.5% per year.

**External relations.** The Belt and Road framework, the AIIB, deepening ties with Russia, and a harder line in maritime disputes. Tsai Ing-wen's election in Taiwan in January 2016 cooled cross-strait relations. China began construction of its first overseas military base in Djibouti.

**Economic basis.** Manufacturing, construction and infrastructure investment, with services rising above half of GDP by official data. "Made in China 2025" (announced May 2015) aimed at upgrading into advanced industries. E-commerce and mobile payments expanded fast, with the National Bureau of Statistics reporting online retail sales of 3,877.3 billion yuan in 2015, up 33.3%.

**Knowledge and technique.** Tu Youyou received the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering artemisinin, the first Nobel science prize for a scientist working in mainland China. China launched the Micius quantum-communications satellite (August 2016), completed the FAST radio telescope (2016), and expanded high-speed rail.

**Key individuals.** Premier Li Keqiang; Wang Qishan; People's Bank of China Governor Zhou Xiaochuan; AIIB President Jin Liqun; entrepreneurs Jack Ma (Alibaba), Pony Ma (Tencent) and Robin Li (Baidu); Didi Chuxing's Cheng Wei, whose firm acquired Uber's China operations in 2016.

**Key organizations.** The Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, the People's Bank of China, the China Securities Regulatory Commission, state-owned policy banks (China Development Bank, Exim Bank), Alibaba, Tencent, Huawei, and the AIIB.

### India

**Internal politics.** The BJP under Narendra Modi won an outright Lok Sabha majority in May 2014. The government pursued investment promotion, bank-account expansion (Jan Dhan), the Goods and Services Tax constitutional amendment (passed 2016), and the November 2016 demonetisation. Debates over religious nationalism, cow-protection violence and academic freedom (e.g., the 2016 controversy at JNU) were intense. The BJP lost the 2015 Bihar and Delhi state elections.

**External relations.** "Act East," expanded ties with the US and Japan, rivalry with China and a hard line on Pakistan after the Pathankot (January 2016) and Uri (September 2016) attacks. India joined the AIIB as a founding member and became its prospective largest borrower.

**Economic basis.** Services (IT and business-process outsourcing), agriculture employing a large share of the workforce, manufacturing and remittances.

**Knowledge and technique.** The Indian Space Research Organisation's Mars Orbiter Mission reached Mars orbit in September 2014 on its first attempt; the Aadhaar biometric identity system passed a billion enrolments in 2016.

**Key individuals.** Finance Minister Arun Jaitley; Reserve Bank of India Governors Raghuraj Rajan (to September 2016) and Urjit Patel; Mukesh Ambani (Reliance Jio); Nandan Nilekani (Aadhaar's architect); opposition leaders Rahul Gandhi, Arvind Kejriwal and Mamata Banerjee.

**Key organizations.** The Reserve Bank of India, NITI Aayog (which replaced the Planning Commission in January 2015), the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, ISRO, Tata and Reliance groups, and Infosys.

### United States

**Internal politics.** Divided government after the 2014 midterms; debates over immigration, policing (the Ferguson protests of 2014 and the Black Lives Matter movement), healthcare and inequality. The 2016 campaign featured insurgent candidacies in both parties. On election eve FiveThirtyEight gave Hillary Clinton about a 70% chance of winning and the Princeton Election Consortium about 93% or higher; Donald Trump won the Electoral College while losing the national popular vote.

**External relations.** The Asia rebalance, the anti-Islamic State coalition, the Iran deal, the Cuba opening, sanctions on Russia and the Paris Agreement.

**Economic basis.** Services, technology and finance, with the shale boom making the US a major oil and gas producer.

**Knowledge and technique.** LIGO's first detection of gravitational waves; SpaceX's first landing of an orbital-class booster (December 2015); leadership in AI research at firms such as Google (DeepMind is UK-based but Google-owned), Facebook and Microsoft.

**Key individuals.** Federal Reserve Chair Janet Yellen; Secretary of State John Kerry; tech leaders such as Apple's Tim Cook (whose 2016 dispute with the FBI over iPhone encryption became a public debate), Amazon's Jeff Bezos and Tesla/SpaceX's Elon Musk.

**Key organizations.** The Federal Reserve, the major technology platforms, US-based multinationals, and civil-society movements.

### Russia and the post-Soviet space

**Internal politics.** Vladimir Putin's approval surged after Crimea's annexation; opposition politician Boris Nemtsov was murdered near the Kremlin in February 2015; State Duma elections in September 2016 strengthened United Russia.

**External relations.** Crimea, the Donbas war (the downing of Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17 in July 2014 killed 298 people), Western sanctions, the Syria intervention, and the Eurasian Economic Union with Kazakhstan, Belarus, Armenia and Kyrgyzstan.

**Economic basis.** Hydrocarbons, metals, arms and grain; the ruble crisis of December 2014 and a 2015 recession forced budget cuts and import substitution.

**Key individuals.** Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov; Bank of Russia Governor Elvira Nabiullina; Defence Minister Sergei Shoigu; Chechen leader Ramzan Kadyrov; opposition activist Alexei Navalny.

**Key organizations.** Gazprom, Rosneft, the Bank of Russia, the armed forces and security services.

**Ukraine.** President Petro Poroshenko (elected May 2014) governed a country fighting separatists in the east while trying to implement an IMF-supported reform programme and an EU Association Agreement. The UN human rights monitoring mission recorded 9,758 people killed and 22,779 injured from mid-April 2014 to 1 December 2016, including over 2,000 civilians plus the 298 killed on MH17.

**Central Asia and the Caucasus.** Kazakhstan and Azerbaijan devalued their currencies in 2015; a flare-up in Nagorno-Karabakh in April 2016 killed dozens; Uzbekistan's long-time ruler Islam Karimov died in September 2016 and was succeeded by Shavkat Mirziyoyev.

### European Union and wider Europe

**Internal politics.** The Juncker Commission; Germany's Chancellor Angela Merkel opened borders to asylum-seekers in 2015 ("Wir schaffen das"); Greek Prime Minister Alexis Tsipras's government called a referendum rejecting bailout terms in July 2015 and then accepted a third bailout; terrorist attacks in Paris (January and November 2015, the latter killing 130), Brussels (March 2016), Nice (July 2016) and Berlin (December 2016); the UK voted 51.9% to 48.1% to leave the EU on 23 June 2016, leading to Theresa May replacing David Cameron.

**External relations.** Sanctions on Russia, the EU–Turkey migration deal, the Iran deal and the Paris Agreement.

**Knowledge and technique.** The European Space Agency's Rosetta mission landed the Philae probe on a comet in November 2014; CERN's Large Hadron Collider restarted at higher energy in 2015.

**Key individuals.** ECB President Mario Draghi; EU foreign-policy chief Federica Mogherini; German Finance Minister Wolfgang Schäuble; Greek Finance Minister Yanis Varoufakis (2015).

**Key organizations.** The European Central Bank, the European Commission, Frontex, and firms such as Volkswagen, whose September 2015 emissions-cheating scandal damaged a flagship industry.

### Japan and the Korean peninsula

Japan's Abe government pursued monetary expansion, security legislation, and outreach to Southeast Asia and India. South Korea faced a MERS outbreak in 2015, the THAAD decision and the Park Geun-hye scandal. North Korea, under Kim Jong-un, conducted nuclear tests in January and September 2016 and multiple missile tests.

### Southeast Asia

ASEAN declared its Economic Community in December 2015. Indonesia under Joko Widodo focused on infrastructure; Thailand's military took power in May 2014, and King Bhumibol Adulyadej died in October 2016 after a 70-year reign; Myanmar's NLD government took office in 2016 while the military retained key powers; Malaysia's 1MDB scandal implicated the Prime Minister's circle; Vietnam balanced between China and the US; the Philippines' Duterte launched a violent anti-drug campaign in which the Philippine National Police counted 2,086 people killed in police operations and 3,841 in extrajudicial or vigilante-style killings between 1 July and 12 December 2016.

### Middle East and North Africa

**Saudi Arabia and the Gulf.** King Salman's accession, the Yemen intervention, Vision 2030 plans to diversify beyond oil, and the leadership of OPEC's November 2014 strategy to defend market share.

**Iran.** The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (July 2015) with Implementation Day in January 2016 lifted nuclear-related sanctions in exchange for limits on enrichment; Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei remained sceptical; Quds Force commander Qasem Soleimani directed Iran's role in Iraq and Syria.

**Turkey.** Erdoğan moved from prime minister to president (August 2014); the peace process with the PKK collapsed in 2015; the July 2016 coup attempt, which the government blamed on the Gülen movement, was followed by mass purges and a state of emergency; Turkey hosted the world's largest refugee population.

**Iraq and Syria.** The Islamic State under Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi seized Mosul in June 2014; Iraqi forces, Popular Mobilisation units, Kurdish Peshmerga and coalition airpower pushed it back from Tikrit, Ramadi and Fallujah and began the Mosul offensive in October 2016. In Syria, Bashar al-Assad's government survived with Russian and Iranian support.

**Egypt.** President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, elected in 2014, presided over repression of the Muslim Brotherhood, the Suez Canal expansion (2015) and an IMF loan with a currency float in November 2016.

**Libya, Tunisia and the Maghreb.** Libya split between rival governments, with the UN-backed Government of National Accord formed after the December 2015 Skhirat agreement; Tunisia's National Dialogue Quartet won the 2015 Nobel Peace Prize despite the Bardo and Sousse attacks.

### Sub-Saharan Africa

**Nigeria.** Buhari's 2015 victory, a military campaign with Chad, Niger and Cameroon that pushed Boko Haram out of most territory it held, the 2014 Chibok schoolgirl abduction, and a 2016 recession driven by oil and militant attacks in the Niger Delta.

**Ethiopia.** A state-led developmental model with high official growth and major infrastructure (the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, the Addis Ababa light rail, the Addis–Djibouti railway), strained by the El Niño drought and by protests in Oromia and Amhara from late 2015, leading to a state of emergency in October 2016 under Prime Minister Hailemariam Desalegn.

**West Africa and the Sahel.** The Ebola epidemic in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone; jihadist violence in Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger despite the French-led Operation Barkhane and the UN mission MINUSMA.

**East Africa and the Great Lakes.** South Sudan's civil war; Burundi's crisis after President Pierre Nkurunziza's third-term bid in 2015; the DRC's postponed elections; al-Shabaab's attacks in Somalia and Kenya (Garissa University, April 2015).

**Southern Africa.** South Africa's slow growth, the #FeesMustFall student movement (2015–16), the Constitutional Court's 2016 Nkandla ruling against President Jacob Zuma, and the ANC's losses of major cities in the August 2016 municipal elections.

**Key individuals.** African Union Commission Chair Nkosazana Dlamini-Zuma; African Development Bank President Akinwumi Adesina (from 2015); Aliko Dangote; Liberia's President Ellen Johnson Sirleaf; Rwanda's Paul Kagame.

### Latin America

**Brazil.** Recession, Lava Jato (investigating a multi-billion-dollar kickback scheme centred on Petrobras and construction firms such as Odebrecht, led by federal judge Sérgio Moro), mass street protests (the largest in March 2016), and the Senate's 61–20 vote on 31 August 2016 removing Rousseff for fiscal-rule violations, with Michel Temer succeeding her. Brazilian political scientists described the impeachment as fitting the Latin American pattern of recession, corruption scandal, loss of legislative support and street protest; Rousseff's supporters called it a parliamentary coup. Rio de Janeiro hosted the 2016 Olympics.

**Venezuela.** President Nicolás Maduro's government faced collapsing oil revenue, shortages and high inflation; the opposition won the National Assembly in December 2015, and a recall referendum drive was halted in October 2016.

**Colombia.** The Havana peace negotiations produced an agreement signed in September 2016; voters rejected it 50.2% to 49.8% on 2 October 2016 with 37.4% turnout; President Juan Manuel Santos received the Nobel Peace Prize; a revised agreement was signed on 24 November and approved by Congress on 29–30 November. Former President Álvaro Uribe led the opposition.

**Argentina, Mexico and Cuba.** Mauricio Macri lifted currency controls and settled with holdout creditors in 2016; Mexico faced the Ayotzinapa disappearances (2014), drug-war violence and a falling peso; Cuba and the US restored relations and Fidel Castro died in November 2016.

## IV. Thematic Sections

### Knowledge, science, and technology

- **Physics and astronomy.** LIGO's announcement of gravitational waves; the Rosetta/Philae comet mission; New Horizons' Pluto flyby (July 2015); China's FAST telescope; the Micius satellite.
- **Artificial intelligence.** Rapid gains in image and speech recognition via deep learning; AlphaGo's 4–1 victory over Lee Sedol in Seoul; large investments by US and Chinese firms (Baidu, Alibaba, Tencent).
- **Biology and medicine.** CRISPR diffusion and patent disputes; the first human-embryo editing experiments in China; the rVSV-ZEBOV Ebola vaccine trial in Guinea, which showed high efficacy in 2015–16; Tu Youyou's Nobel for artemisinin.
- **Space.** India's Mars mission; SpaceX and Blue Origin booster landings; China's Tiangong-2 space lab (2016).
- **Digital economy.** Mobile payments in China, India's Jio disruption and the Aadhaar platform, Africa's mobile money, ride-hailing and "platform" firms globally.

### Health and population

- **Ebola (West Africa).** WHO reported 28,616 confirmed, probable and suspected cases and 11,310 deaths in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone, with 36 further cases in seven other countries; WHO believed the true toll was higher. WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on 8 August 2014 and ended it on 29 March 2016. Incidence peaked in autumn 2014. Inside the affected countries, community distrust, burial practices, weak health systems and the mobility of populations shaped transmission, and local health workers, community engagement and safe-burial teams were central to bringing it under control. In September 2014 the US CDC modelled a worst case of up to 1.4 million cases by January 2015 if interventions failed — a projection that shaped the massive international scale-up.
- **Zika.** A WHO emergency declared in February 2016 over microcephaly clusters linked to Zika in Brazil.
- **MERS.** A hospital-centred outbreak in South Korea (2015).
- **Other.** Yellow fever in Angola and the DRC (2016); cholera in Yemen (from October 2016); the Syrian health system's collapse; the Sustainable Development Goals' health targets.

### Security and conflict

- **Syria.** Death-toll estimates were contested: the UN stopped counting in 2014 at about 250,000; UN envoy Staffan de Mistura estimated about 400,000 in April 2016, stressing it was not an official figure; the Syrian Center for Policy Research estimated 470,000 direct and indirect deaths as of early 2016.
- **Iraq.** The war against the Islamic State, with the Mosul offensive underway at end-2016.
- **Yemen.** The Houthi takeover of Sanaa (September 2014), the Saudi-led intervention (March 2015) and a UN-brokered peace effort that collapsed in August 2016. OHCHR counted at least 4,125 civilians killed and 7,207 wounded from March 2015 to 10 October 2016, the majority by coalition airstrikes; UN Humanitarian Coordinator Jamie McGoldrick said on 30 August 2016 that at least 10,000 people had been killed in total, up from about 6,000, based on health-facility data, adding: "We know the numbers are much higher but we can't tell you by how much."
- **Ukraine.** 9,758 killed per OHCHR by 1 December 2016.
- **Afghanistan and Pakistan.** NATO ended its combat mission in December 2014; the Taliban seized Kunduz briefly in 2015; Pakistan's army conducted the Zarb-e-Azb operation after the Peshawar school massacre (December 2014).
- **Africa.** The Boko Haram insurgency, South Sudan, the Sahel, Somalia, Libya.
- **Terrorism.** Islamic State and al-Qaeda-inspired attacks in Europe, Turkey, the Middle East, Africa and South Asia.
- **Military technique.** Wide use of drones by states and non-state actors; Russian stand-off cruise missiles fired from the Caspian into Syria (2015); information operations; anti-access/area-denial capabilities in the Western Pacific.

### Culture and society

- **Mobile and social media.** The spread of WeChat, WhatsApp and Facebook shaped politics from the Hong Kong protests to elections in India, the Philippines and the US; debates over "fake news" and filter bubbles emerged by late 2016.
- **Religion.** Pope Francis's Laudato si'; sectarian conflict in the Middle East; religious nationalism in India, Myanmar and elsewhere.
- **Culture.** Svetlana Alexievich (Belarus) won the 2015 Nobel in Literature and Bob Dylan the 2016 prize; Korean pop culture spread across Asia; Nollywood and Bollywood reached global audiences; Pokémon Go (2016) became a global phenomenon.
- **Social movements.** Black Lives Matter, #FeesMustFall, anti-corruption protests in Brazil, Guatemala (where President Otto Pérez Molina resigned in 2015) and South Korea, and LGBT rights advances (e.g., the US Supreme Court's 2015 Obergefell ruling) alongside restrictions elsewhere.

## V. Conclusion

At end-2016 the world stood at an open juncture. The global economy was growing but slowly, with the IMF and most forecasters projecting modest recovery in 2017 while warning of downside risks from China, commodity exporters, protectionism and the UK's EU exit. China had become a builder of institutions and the main alternative source of development finance, but faced debt and capital-flight risks that analysts debated intensely. Russia had shown willingness to use force in Ukraine and Syria; the Middle East was in the most violent phase of its post-2011 turmoil; and Africa's largest economies were in recession, even as democratic transfers of power in Nigeria and elsewhere offered counter-signals. Displacement, epidemics and climate stress were recognised as global rather than regional problems, and the Paris Agreement and SDGs represented a genuine multilateral response. The central open questions for contemporaries were whether China's rebalancing would succeed without a hard landing; whether the incoming US administration would retreat from alliances and trade agreements; whether the EU could manage the UK's departure and migration pressures; whether the Islamic State's loss of territory would reduce or disperse jihadist violence; whether oil producers' new coordination would hold; and whether the new populism in democracies was a passing reaction or a durable realignment.
