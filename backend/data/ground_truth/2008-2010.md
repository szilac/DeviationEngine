# Ground-Truth World-State Report: 2008 to 2010

## I. Executive Summary

Between 2008 and 2010 the world economy went through a financial shock that began in US mortgage credit and North Atlantic banking. It became global in September–October 2008, after Lehman Brothers failed and credit markets froze, and global trade collapsed in late 2008 and early 2009. The IMF's estimates kept changing:
- January 2009: world growth of ½% for 2009.
- April 2009: a 1.3% decline in 2009.
- October 2010: the contraction had been milder, and 2010 growth would be about 4.8%.

In October 2010 the IMF described a "Great Recession" in which "most advanced economies… are recovering only sluggishly," while "many emerging and developing economies are again seeing strong growth."

**The response widened who sat at the table.** The G20 met at leaders' level for the first time in Washington in November 2008, then in London, Pittsburgh, Toronto and Seoul. China's State Council announced a RMB 4 trillion (about US$586 billion) stimulus on 9 November 2008, mostly investment, to be spent through end-2010. China became the most visible engine of recovery. In the second quarter of 2010, Japanese Cabinet Office data showed China's dollar-denominated output exceeding Japan's, and full-year confirmation was expected in early 2011.

**The crisis then passed into public finance.**
- Late 2009: Greece admitted misstated deficits.
- May 2010: Greece received a €110 billion EU–IMF package, and a €750 billion euro-area stabilization framework was created.
- Late November 2010: Ireland, weighed down by its guarantees of its banks, agreed a €67.5 billion external package (€85 billion including domestic resources).
- By December 2010, Portugal and Spain were openly discussed as the next pressure points.

**Parallel crises:**
- **Food.** The 2007–08 food and fuel price spike set off unrest in dozens of countries. The FAO estimated that hunger passed one billion people in 2009. In August 2010 Russia's drought led Moscow to ban grain exports, and prices rose again.
- **Pandemic.** A new influenza A(H1N1) virus appeared in Mexico and the US in spring 2009. WHO declared a pandemic on 11 June 2009 and declared it over in August 2010. At least 18,449 laboratory-confirmed deaths were reported, an acknowledged undercount.
- **Disasters.** Cyclone Nargis and the Sichuan earthquake (May 2008); Haiti's earthquake (January 2010), with a disputed toll of roughly 100,000–316,000 dead; Chile's magnitude-8.8 earthquake (February 2010); and Pakistan's floods (July–August 2010), which affected about 20 million people.

**Politics.**
- The US elected Barack Obama.
- Japan's Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) lost power to the Democratic Party of Japan (DPJ) in 2009.
- Russia ran as a Medvedev–Putin "tandem."
- India's United Progressive Alliance (UPA) coalition was re-elected in 2009.
- Brazil elected Dilma Rousseff in October 2010.
- Iran's June 2009 election set off its largest protests since 1979.
- In Tunisia, a street vendor set himself on fire in Sidi Bouzid on 17 December 2010. The protests that followed were still spreading at year's end.

**Overall characterization:** the world at the end of 2010 was multipolar in economic weight but still anchored in US military and monetary power. It was recovering at two speeds, was more interconnected by mobile telephony and the internet than ever, and was carrying unresolved fiscal, food-security and regional-security stresses whose outcomes no one could yet see.

## II. Global Overview

### Distribution of power

**The United States** remained the only power with global military reach and the issuer of the dominant reserve currency. The crisis had begun in its financial system, however, which damaged the prestige of its economic model. It was drawing down in Iraq under the 2008 Status of Forces Agreement (combat mission declared ended 31 August 2010) while adding 30,000 troops in Afghanistan (announced December 2009). The Obama administration pursued a "reset" with Russia (New START signed April 2010) and engagement with Iran. It also had to manage China as both creditor and competitor.

**China** under Hu Jintao and Wen Jiabao was, by contemporary judgment, the pivotal rising economic power. It was the largest holder of US Treasuries and the largest exporter of manufactures, governed by a party-state working through state banks and state enterprises. In 2009 central bank governor Zhou Xiaochuan floated a super-sovereign reserve currency based on the IMF's Special Drawing Rights (SDR), and Beijing began piloting trade settlement in renminbi. Relations with Japan were strained by the September 2010 Senkaku/Diaoyu collision, and China declined to condemn North Korea over the Cheonan sinking.

**The European Union** had 27 members. The Lisbon Treaty took effect on 1 December 2009, creating a permanent European Council president (Herman Van Rompuy) and a foreign-policy High Representative (Catherine Ashton). The 16-member euro area was a monetary union without a common treasury, a gap that 2010 exposed. Germany (Merkel) and France (Sarkozy) were decisive in managing the crisis.

**Russia** had rebuilt state capacity in the oil boom and claimed "privileged interests" in the former Soviet space. After the August 2008 war it recognized Abkhazia and South Ossetia, and its hydrocarbon-dependent economy contracted sharply in 2009.

**Japan** remained the second-largest economy by dollar measures until mid-2010 and a technology leader, marked by deflation, ageing, high public debt and rapid turnover of prime ministers.

**India** grew on services and domestic demand, and the 2008 US–India nuclear deal raised its profile.

**Brazil** under Lula combined commodity exports, social transfers and new "pre-salt" oil, found in deep offshore fields beneath a thick layer of salt. In May 2010 Brazil and Turkey jointly mediated on Iran's nuclear program.

**New groupings.** The BRIC countries (Brazil, Russia, India, China) held their first summit in Yekaterinburg in June 2009, and China invited South Africa to join in late 2010. The BASIC group (Brazil, South Africa, India, China), formed in November 2009, negotiated the Copenhagen Accord directly with the United States.

**Regional powers with substantial autonomy** included Turkey (under the Justice and Development Party, AKP, and Foreign Minister Davutoğlu's "zero problems with neighbours" policy), Iran, Saudi Arabia and the Gulf monarchies, Indonesia under Yudhoyono, South Africa, Nigeria, Mexico and South Korea, host of the G20 in November 2010.

### Economy and exchange

**Production and trade.** Manufacturing was concentrated in East Asia. China assembled components from Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Southeast Asia for North American and European markets. The Gulf, Russia, West Africa, Brazil and Australia supplied energy and raw materials. South Asia exported services and workers; those workers' remittances sustained South Asia, Central America, the Philippines and North Africa. IMF and World Bank estimates at the time put the fall in world trade volume in 2009 at more than 10%.

**Commodity prices** swung violently. Oil (WTI) hit a record $147.27 a barrel on 11 July 2008, fell to about $32 in December 2008, and recovered to about $80–90 by late 2010. The FAO food price index peaked in mid-2008 and climbed again in late 2010.

**The financial crisis** ran through US subprime securities, "shadow banking" (credit supplied outside regulated deposit-taking banks) and derivatives.
- March 2008: Bear Stearns was rescued.
- September 2008: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were taken into conservatorship, Lehman failed and AIG was rescued.
- October 2008: Congress passed the $700 billion Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP). Iceland's three big banks collapsed, and European governments recapitalized and guaranteed their banks.
- Hungary, Latvia, Ukraine and Romania turned to the IMF.

Central banks cut rates to near zero. The Federal Reserve bought assets on a large scale ("quantitative easing") in 2008–09 and announced a further $600 billion in November 2010. In September 2010 Brazil's finance minister Guido Mantega called the resulting cross-currents a "currency war."

**Regional finance.**
- Dubai World sought a debt standstill in November 2009 and needed support from Abu Dhabi.
- The ASEAN+3 countries turned the Chiang Mai Initiative into a $120 billion multilateral reserve pool (effective 2010).
- The ASEAN–China Free Trade Area took full effect for the original ASEAN members in 2010.

**Aggregate figures (vintage vs. revised).** The IMF's October 2010 estimate for 2009 was a world contraction of about 0.6% in PPP terms, with advanced economies down 3% or more. The contraction was larger at market exchange rates, and later PPP revisions changed the numbers, so the plausible range is roughly −0.1% to −2%.
- Euro area: −4.0% (UN DESA, mid-2010).
- Japan: about −5% to −6%.
- China: officially about +9%.
- Russia: about −8%.
- Baltic states: double-digit contractions.
- US unemployment peaked at 10.0% in October 2009.

Figures for informal-economy-dominated states in Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Asia and conflict zones are especially uncertain.

### Social and demographic conditions

**Population.** UN estimates put world population at about 6.9 billion in 2010, with 7 billion projected for late 2011. By UN reckoning about half of humanity was urban by around 2008, with the fastest growth in Asia and Africa. China and India each had more than a billion people. China's November 2010 census results were not yet published at year's end.

**Migration.** China's internal migrant workforce numbered in the low hundreds of millions. On 2 February 2009, Chen Xiwen, director of the Office of the Central Leading Group on Rural Work, said an estimated 20 million migrant workers had lost jobs or returned home without work because of the global financial crisis. By 2010 coastal labor shortages and strikes had appeared instead, for example at a Honda supplier in Guangdong in May–June 2010. Remittances to developing countries fell in 2009.

**Displacement** affected Iraqis in Syria and Jordan, Afghans in Pakistan and Iran, Somalis in Kenya and Sudanese in Chad. Hundreds of thousands more were displaced in Sri Lanka, Pakistan's Swat valley (2009) and Kyrgyzstan (2010), and after Haiti's earthquake and Pakistan's floods.

**Food insecurity.** The FAO estimated 1.02 billion undernourished people in 2009. The World Bank estimated that the food-price surge pushed 130–155 million people into poverty in 2008.

**Mobile phones** spread extremely fast in India, China and Sub-Saharan Africa, reshaping everyday exchange.

### Environment and natural events

**Climate diplomacy.** The Copenhagen conference (December 2009) produced no binding successor to the Kyoto Protocol. The US and the BASIC states instead brokered the Copenhagen Accord, which the plenary only "took note" of. The Accord endorsed a 2 °C goal and voluntary pledges, including, for the first time, from major developing economies. The Cancún conference (December 2010) brought much of it into the formal UN process.

**Disasters:**
- May 2008: Cyclone Nargis in Myanmar. The official count in the Post-Nargis Joint Assessment (Tripartite Core Group of the Myanmar government, ASEAN and the UN, July 2008) was 84,537 dead and 53,836 missing.
- May 2008: the Sichuan earthquake. Final official figures (25 August 2008) were 69,227 dead, 17,923 missing and 374,643 injured.
- 2009: Typhoons Morakot (Taiwan) and Ketsana (Philippines).
- January 2010: the Haiti earthquake. Haitian government figures rose from about 222,000 to 316,000 dead or missing; independent studies estimated roughly 100,000–160,000.
- February 2010: Chile's earthquake and tsunami.
- April 2010: Iceland's Eyjafjallajökull eruption closed much of European airspace, and the Deepwater Horizon rig blowout spilled oil into the Gulf of Mexico.
- Summer 2010: Russia's heatwave, drought and wildfires. The agriculture minister said 43 regions were affected, and the grain harvest fell by roughly a quarter to a third.
- July–August 2010: Pakistan's floods, with about 1,985 deaths and 20 million people affected according to Pakistan's National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA), and about one-fifth of the country's land area affected.
- From October 2010: a cholera epidemic in Haiti.

### Flashpoints of the period (as seen at end-2010)

1. **Euro-area sovereign debt.** It was unclear whether the May 2010 framework and the Irish package would contain contagion to Portugal and Spain. EU leaders agreed in principle to create a permanent crisis mechanism.
2. **The Korean peninsula.**
   - The corvette Cheonan sank in March 2010 with 46 sailors lost. A South Korean-led international investigation blamed a North Korean torpedo; Pyongyang denied it.
   - North Korea shelled Yeonpyeong island on 23 November 2010, killing four South Koreans, and revealed a uranium enrichment facility the same month.
   - Kim Jong-un was publicly elevated as heir in September 2010.
3. **Iran's nuclear program.** The Western powers rejected the Brazil–Turkey fuel-swap initiative, and the UN Security Council passed Resolution 1929 (June 2010). Tensions with Israel ran high, and the Stuxnet worm was reported in 2010.
4. **Afghanistan–Pakistan.** The NATO surge followed a disputed Afghan election (August 2009). Pakistani offensives in Swat and South Waziristan (2009) coincided with US drone strikes, and India–Pakistan dialogue stayed frozen after Mumbai.
5. **Sudan.** A southern self-determination referendum was due in January 2011 under the 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement, with the status of Abyei and oil revenue-sharing unresolved.

Also watched closely: the Côte d'Ivoire standoff, the Tunisian protests, the US–China exchange-rate dispute and food prices.

### Emerging forces (judged as a contemporary might)

- **Smartphones and the mobile internet.** Apple's App Store opened in 2008, Android arrived from late 2008 and the iPad in 2010. Social networks were visible in Iran's and Moldova's 2009 protests, and their political weight was debated.
- **Mobile money.** Kenya's M-Pesa, launched in 2007 by the operator Safaricom, had about 9.5 million users by March 2010.
- **Chinese state finance abroad.** Policy banks and pledges through the Forum on China–Africa Cooperation (FOCAC, November 2009) extended lending to Africa, Latin America and Central Asia.
- **Chinese high-speed rail.** The Wuhan–Guangzhou line opened in December 2009.
- **New forums:** the G20 at leaders' level, BRIC and BASIC, and Asian reserve pooling.
- **Unconventional energy.** US shale gas was rising fast, though its lasting significance was debated. Wind and solar were growing on the scale of Chinese manufacturing. In December 2010 Brazil's Tupi pre-salt field was declared commercial and renamed Lula.
- **Digital identity.** India's Unique Identification Authority (UIDAI), created in 2009, issued its first Aadhaar identity numbers in 2010.
- **WikiLeaks** released US military logs and, from 28 November 2010, State Department cables.
- **Bitcoin.** The whitepaper appeared in October 2008 and the network launched in January 2009. It was an obscure curiosity in 2010.

### Systemic fragilities

- **Banks and states.** Bank leverage and interconnection, with sovereign guarantees tying state solvency to banks (Ireland, Iceland), and a euro area without fiscal union.
- **Global imbalances.** US deficits against East Asian, German and oil-exporter surpluses, with the dollar system resting on continued Chinese and Gulf accumulation of dollar assets.
- **Food-system concentration.** Export bans by a few grain and rice exporters passed shocks to importers such as Egypt.
- **Oil chokepoints.** Somali piracy peaked in 2008–2010 around the Gulf of Aden and Bab el-Mandeb, drawing naval patrols from the EU, NATO, the US-led Combined Task Force 151 (CTF-151), China, India, Russia and Japan.
- **Youth unemployment and food-price exposure** in North Africa and the Middle East under long-tenured rulers (Ben Ali since 1987, Mubarak since 1981, Gaddafi since 1969, Saleh since 1978), several facing succession questions.
- **Epidemic exposure** through air travel, alongside persistent HIV/AIDS and cholera.
- **Monsoon, flood and seismic exposure** of dense populations.

## III. Major Powers and Regional Blocs

### China (People's Republic of China)

**Internal politics.**
- The Hu–Wen leadership's slogans were "harmonious society" and "scientific development."
- 2008 brought severe winter storms, unrest in Tibetan areas in March, the Sichuan earthquake, the Beijing Olympics and the melamine-contaminated milk scandal.
- In July 2009, violence in Urumqi killed about 197 people by official count, and Xinjiang's internet was cut for months.
- In 2010 the jailed dissident Liu Xiaobo, an author of the Charter 08 manifesto, won the Nobel Peace Prize, which Beijing denounced.
- In October 2010 Xi Jinping was made vice-chairman of the Central Military Commission, read as positioning him for the undecided 2012 succession.

**External relations.** China presented itself as a responsible stakeholder. It sent naval escorts to the Gulf of Aden from December 2008 and hosted the stalled Six-Party Talks on North Korea. Maritime disputes with Japan and Southeast Asian claimants sharpened in 2010.

**Economic basis.**
- Exports fell sharply in late 2008.
- World Bank researchers put the RMB 4 trillion stimulus at about 12.5% of 2008 GDP. The central government contributed about RMB 1.18 trillion; the rest came from local governments, state enterprises and banks, amid a surge in lending.
- By 2010 debt run up by local-government financing vehicles was drawing warnings.
- Official growth was about 9% in 2009 and above 10% in 2010.
- The renminbi was effectively re-pegged to the dollar from mid-2008 until June 2010, then allowed to appreciate gradually.

**Knowledge and technique.** Shenzhou-7's first spacewalk (2008); the Chang'e-2 lunar probe (2010); the Tianhe-1A supercomputer, ranked first in the world in November 2010; high-speed rail; and the world's largest internet population.

**Key individuals.**
- Vice-President Xi Jinping and Vice-Premier Li Keqiang, the presumed successors.
- Central bank governor Zhou Xiaochuan.
- Vice-Premier Wang Qishan, who ran China's side of the Strategic and Economic Dialogue with the US.
- Chongqing party chief Bo Xilai.
- Artist Ai Weiwei, who investigated schoolchildren's deaths in the Sichuan earthquake.

**Key organizations** included the Politburo Standing Committee, the State Council, the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC), the People's Bank of China, the state-asset regulator SASAC and state enterprises (Sinopec, CNPC, China Mobile, State Grid), China Development Bank, China Investment Corporation, and private firms Huawei, Alibaba and Tencent.

### United States

**Internal politics.** The Bush administration's final year was consumed by the crisis. Under Obama, Congress passed the $787 billion Recovery Act (February 2009), and General Motors and Chrysler went through government-managed bankruptcies. Health-insurance reform followed in March 2010 and the Dodd–Frank financial reform in July 2010. The Tea Party movement emerged in 2009, and Republicans won the House in November 2010.

**Economic basis.** The largest economy, with losses concentrated in housing and finance, unemployment near 10%, and a greatly expanded Federal Reserve balance sheet.

**Knowledge and technique.** Dominance in software and the internet (Google, Apple, Facebook, Amazon), biotechnology and universities. A cell with a synthetic genome (J. Craig Venter Institute, May 2010) and NASA's Kepler planet-hunting telescope (2009).

**Key individuals.** Treasury Secretaries Henry Paulson and Timothy Geithner, Federal Reserve Chair Ben Bernanke, Secretary of State Hillary Clinton, Defense Secretary Robert Gates, and Generals David Petraeus and Stanley McChrystal.

### European Union and the euro area

**Internal politics.** Ireland's second referendum (October 2009) cleared the Lisbon Treaty. In 2010 governments turned to austerity, and protests followed in Greece, France (over pensions), Spain, Portugal and Ireland. In Britain, Gordon Brown's government recapitalized the banks in 2008, and a Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition under David Cameron took office in May 2010.

**External relations.**
- Sarkozy mediated the Russia–Georgia ceasefire, and the EU commissioned the Tagliavini inquiry into the war.
- The EU led Operation Atalanta against Somali piracy and launched the Eastern Partnership (2009).
- Russia–Ukraine gas disputes cut supplies to parts of Europe in January 2009.

**Economic basis.** The largest trading bloc. German exports rebounded strongly in 2010, while the periphery (Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain) ran high deficits. The Baltic states and Hungary went through deep adjustment.

**Knowledge and technique.** CERN's Large Hadron Collider began high-energy collisions in March 2010, after a damaging incident in 2008. Manchester's Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov won the 2010 physics Nobel for graphene.

**Key individuals and organizations.** European Central Bank (ECB) President Jean-Claude Trichet, Eurogroup chair Jean-Claude Juncker, German Finance Minister Wolfgang Schäuble, Greek Prime Minister George Papandreou, Irish Finance Minister Brian Lenihan and IMF Managing Director Dominique Strauss-Kahn. The ECB, the European Commission and the new European Financial Stability Facility (EFSF).

### Russia (Russian Federation)

**Internal politics.** Dmitry Medvedev became president in May 2008, with Vladimir Putin as prime minister and still the dominant figure. Medvedev promoted "modernization," including the Skolkovo innovation hub. Moscow mayor Yuri Luzhkov was dismissed in September 2010. The North Caucasus insurgency continued, including the March 2010 Moscow metro bombings.

**External relations.** The EU-commissioned Tagliavini report (September 2009) found that Georgia's shelling of Tskhinvali on the night of 7–8 August 2008 began large-scale hostilities. It also found that Russia's response went beyond reasonable self-defense and violated international law. Both governments read the report selectively.
- Russia signed New START with the US.
- After Viktor Yanukovych's 2010 election in Ukraine, the Kharkiv agreements extended Russia's lease on the Black Sea Fleet base in Crimea.
- Russia led the Collective Security Treaty Organization (CSTO) and co-led the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) with China.

**Economic basis.** Oil, gas, metals and grain. The stock market crashed in 2008, the ruble was devalued in a managed way, and grain exports were banned in 2010.

**Key individuals and organizations.** Finance Minister Alexei Kudrin, Deputy Prime Minister Igor Sechin, Gazprom chief Alexei Miller, Chechen leader Ramzan Kadyrov and opposition leader Boris Nemtsov. Gazprom, Rosneft, Sberbank and the ruling United Russia party.

### Japan

**Internal politics.** In August 2009 the DPJ won a landslide, ending more than half a century of almost uninterrupted LDP rule. Prime Minister Hatoyama Yukio resigned in June 2010, partly over relocating the US Marine air station at Futenma in Okinawa. His successor Kan Naoto beat back a party challenge from Ozawa Ichirō in September 2010.

**External relations.** Strain with the US over Okinawa. In September 2010 a dispute with China over a detained trawler captain near the Senkakus was followed by reported Chinese restrictions on rare-earth exports.

**Economic basis.** Exports of cars, electronics and capital goods collapsed in 2008–09. Deflation persisted, and Japan intervened against a strong yen in September 2010.

**Knowledge and technique.** The Hayabusa asteroid sample return (June 2010), Yamanaka Shinya's induced pluripotent stem-cell research at Kyoto, and the 2010 chemistry Nobel for Negishi Ei-ichi and Suzuki Akira.

### India (Republic of India)

**Internal politics.**
- Manmohan Singh's UPA survived a 2008 confidence vote over the nuclear deal and won a larger mandate in May 2009.
- The prime minister called the Maoist insurgency India's greatest internal security threat. In April 2010 an ambush at Dantewada killed 76 security personnel.
- Kashmir saw mass street protests in summer 2010.
- The Commonwealth Games (October 2010) were marred by scandal, and a controversy over 2G telecom licensing broke in late 2010.

**External relations.** The Nuclear Suppliers Group granted the 2008 waiver that made the US–India nuclear deal possible. Dialogue with Pakistan was suspended after Mumbai. India took part in BASIC, hosted Obama in November 2010 and concluded a goods free-trade agreement with ASEAN.

**Economic basis.** Information technology and business-process outsourcing, pharmaceuticals and monsoon-dependent agriculture (the 2009 monsoon was weak). Contemporary official data showed growth of about 6–7% in fiscal 2008–09, recovering to about 8–9% in 2010–11.

**Knowledge and technique.** The Chandrayaan-1 lunar orbiter (October 2008) carried NASA's Moon Mineralogy Mapper, which found signs of water or hydroxyl on the lunar surface. Other developments were Aadhaar and 752.19 million wireless subscribers at end-December 2010, up from 729.57 million a month earlier (Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, TRAI).

**Key individuals.** Congress president Sonia Gandhi, Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee, Home Minister P. Chidambaram, Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Governor D. Subbarao, UIDAI chair Nandan Nilekani, industrialists Ratan Tata and Mukesh Ambani, BJP leaders L.K. Advani and Narendra Modi, and Mamata Banerjee in West Bengal.

**Key organizations.** The RBI, the Planning Commission, the Tata group, Reliance, Infosys, the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), and the National Investigation Agency created after Mumbai.

### Brazil and Latin America

**Brazil.** Lula's second term ended with very high approval ratings, and Dilma Rousseff of the Workers' Party beat José Serra in the October 2010 runoff.
- Brazil led the UN stabilization mission in Haiti (MINUSTAH), co-sponsored the May 2010 Tehran declaration, and was active in the G20, BRIC, BASIC, the Union of South American Nations (UNASUR, founded 2008) and the WTO.
- Its economy rested on soy, iron ore (Vale), meat, sugar and ethanol, alongside the Bolsa Família cash-transfer program.
- A 2010 law created a production-sharing regime for pre-salt oil.
- Growth rebounded strongly in 2010. The national statistics institute (IBGE) later put it at about 7.5%, a figure not yet published at end-2010.
- Key figures and institutions: Finance Minister Guido Mantega, central bank president Henrique Meirelles, Petrobras chief José Sérgio Gabrielli, the development bank BNDES, Petrobras and Vale.

**Mexico (Calderón).** In January 2011 the government released an official count of 34,612 organized-crime-related killings from December 2006 to December 2010, including 15,273 in 2010 alone. Other counts differ: the newspaper Reforma counted 11,583 for 2010, and the national statistics agency INEGI recorded 24,374 homicides of all kinds in 2010. Mexico was also where the H1N1 outbreak was first identified.

**Elsewhere in the region.**
- Venezuela (Chávez) built oil-funded alliances through ALBA.
- Bolivia (Morales) adopted a new constitution in 2009.
- Colombia (Uribe, then Santos from August 2010): a March 2008 raid into Ecuador killed FARC commander Raúl Reyes and set off a regional crisis.
- Honduras: President Zelaya was removed in a coup in June 2009.
- Chile: Piñera took office just after the earthquake, and 33 trapped miners were rescued in October 2010.
- Argentina: the Kirchners clashed with farmers and nationalized private pension funds (2008), and Néstor Kirchner died in October 2010.

### Middle East and North Africa

**Iran.** On 12 June 2009, officials announced that Ahmadinejad had won 62.63% of the vote to Mousavi's 33.75%. Hundreds of thousands protested under the slogan "Where is my vote?" Supreme Leader Khamenei endorsed the result, and the Guardian Council said a partial recount confirmed it. Mass arrests and show trials followed. The "Green Movement" around Mousavi and Karroubi was suppressed but not dissolved. Abroad, Iran continued enrichment, negotiated over fuel for the Tehran research reactor (2009–10) and came under new UN sanctions in June 2010. Key actors beyond the Supreme Leader included the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), Rafsanjani and parliament speaker Larijani.

**Iraq.** Violence was down from its 2006–07 peak but still high. The March 2010 election produced eight months of deadlock, ended in November by the Erbil power-sharing deal. It kept Nouri al-Maliki as prime minister and left Iyad Allawi's Iraqiya bloc aggrieved.

**Israel–Palestine.** The Gaza war ran from 27 December 2008 to 18 January 2009.
- Palestinian deaths: B'Tselem counted 1,387, including 773 who took no part in hostilities (320 of them minors). The Palestinian Centre for Human Rights (PCHR) counted 1,417. The Israeli military said 1,166, most of them militants.
- Israeli deaths: 13, comprising 10 soldiers (four by friendly fire) and 3 civilians.
- The UN-commissioned Goldstone report (September 2009) alleged violations by both sides.

Netanyahu returned as prime minister in 2009. A partial settlement freeze ran from November 2009 to September 2010, and direct talks collapsed after it. Israel's interception of a Gaza-bound flotilla on 31 May 2010 killed nine people aboard the Mavi Marmara and ruptured relations with Turkey. Hamas governed Gaza. In the West Bank, under the Fatah-led Palestinian Authority, Prime Minister Salam Fayyad pursued state-building.

**Turkey.** The AKP survived a court case seeking its closure in 2008. The Ergenekon trials of alleged coup plotters continued, a constitutional referendum passed on 12 September 2010, and Turkish diplomacy and trade expanded across the Middle East, Africa and the Balkans.

**Gulf.** Oil revenues dipped and recovered. Dubai's debt shock hit in 2009, after Gulf sovereign funds had helped recapitalize Western banks in 2008. Qatar won the right to host the 2022 World Cup (December 2010), and Saudi Arabia fought Houthi forces on its border in 2009–10.

**Egypt.** Food-price protests and the 6 April youth movement, uncertainty over succession, and a late-2010 parliamentary election widely called rigged. Egypt depended heavily on imported wheat.

**Tunisia.** A 2008 revolt in the Gafsa mining basin preceded the protests that began in Sidi Bouzid on 17 December 2010. Driven by unemployment, police abuse and regional neglect, they had spread by month's end with the outcome unknown.

### Sub-Saharan Africa

**Nigeria.** President Yar'Adua's long illness created a constitutional crisis. In February 2010 the National Assembly invoked a "doctrine of necessity" to make Vice-President Goodluck Jonathan acting president, and Jonathan succeeded when Yar'Adua died in May. A 2009 amnesty reduced militant attacks in the Niger Delta. A July 2009 uprising by the Boko Haram sect in Maiduguri ended with its leader, Mohammed Yusuf, killed in police custody, and the group's attacks resumed in late 2010.

**South Africa.** The ANC recalled President Mbeki in September 2008, and Jacob Zuma became president in May 2009. South Africa entered recession in 2009, hosted the 2010 World Cup (the first in Africa) and joined BASIC.

**Kenya.** The National Accord brokered by Kofi Annan in February 2008 ended post-election violence that had killed about 1,100 people. A new constitution passed by referendum on 4 August 2010 with 66.9% of all votes cast (68.55% of valid votes). In December 2010 the International Criminal Court (ICC) prosecutor named six suspects.

**Zimbabwe.** Official inflation reached 231 million percent in July 2008 (Central Statistical Office, reported via state media). Unofficial estimates, notably Steve Hanke's, ran into the sextillions of percent. Zimbabwe's main parties signed the Global Political Agreement on 15 September 2008, and a unity government with opposition leader Morgan Tsvangirai as prime minister was sworn in on 11 February 2009. The country effectively adopted the US dollar and the South African rand in 2009, and cholera spread in 2008–09.

**Sudan.** The ICC issued arrest warrants for President al-Bashir in March 2009 and July 2010; the second added genocide charges. Elections were held in April 2010, and the southern referendum was due in January 2011. Conflict in Darfur continued at lower intensity, with the joint UN–African Union mission (UNAMID) deployed.

**Horn of Africa.** Ethiopia withdrew its troops from Somalia in January 2009. Somalia's Transitional Federal Government relied on the African Union mission (AMISOM). Al-Shabaab's July 2010 bombings in Kampala extended the conflict beyond Somalia.

**West Africa and beyond: coups and contested elections.**
- Coups or unconstitutional seizures of power occurred in Mauritania (2008), Guinea (December 2008), Madagascar (2009) and Niger (February 2010).
- In Côte d'Ivoire, the 28 November 2010 runoff produced rival results. The Independent Electoral Commission announced a win for Alassane Ouattara with 54.10%, and the UN certified it. The Constitutional Council, headed by an appointee of incumbent Laurent Gbagbo, cancelled votes from northern regions (400,000–500,000 by different counts) and proclaimed Gbagbo the winner with 51.45%.
- Both men took oaths on 4 December. The African Union and the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) backed Ouattara, and the standoff was unresolved.

**Economic basis.** Oil (Nigeria, Angola), minerals (South Africa, Congo, Zambian copper), cocoa, coffee and remittances. Chinese, Indian, Brazilian and Gulf investment was rising, and mobile phones were spreading fast. The IMF's vintage estimate for 2009 regional growth was roughly 2–3%, on weak data.

**Key organizations.** The African Union Commission (Jean Ping), ECOWAS, the Southern African Development Community (SADC), the African Development Bank, and the firms Safaricom, MTN and Dangote.

### South and Southeast Asia (beyond India)

**Pakistan.** President Musharraf resigned in August 2008, and Asif Ali Zardari succeeded him. The Islamabad Marriott was bombed in September 2008, and an IMF program followed in November. The lawyers' movement won the restoration of Chief Justice Iftikhar Chaudhry in March 2009. The army retook Swat in 2009, and the 2010 floods followed.

**Sri Lanka.** Government forces defeated the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE) in May 2009, and their leader Velupillai Prabhakaran was killed. Civilian deaths in the final months are sharply contested:
- A contemporaneous UN working estimate was about 6,500 from mid-January to mid-April 2009.
- Later UN estimates run from about 40,000 to 70,000 or more.
- The government initially claimed near-zero civilian casualties.

About 280,000–300,000 displaced Tamils were held in camps. President Rajapaksa won re-election in January 2010, and his rival, former army commander Sarath Fonseka, was imprisoned.

**Myanmar.** The military regime held its constitutional referendum days after Nargis. The main opposition National League for Democracy (NLD) boycotted the November 2010 election, which was widely dismissed. Aung San Suu Kyi was released from house arrest on 13 November 2010. How far the nominal transition would go was unknown.

**Thailand.** In late 2008, "yellow-shirt" protesters of the People's Alliance for Democracy (PAD) occupied Bangkok's airports. In April–May 2010, "red-shirt" protests ended in a military crackdown with at least 90 dead and more than 2,000 injured, the figures later documented by Thailand's Truth for Reconciliation Commission.

**Indonesia.** President Yudhoyono was re-elected in 2009. Growth of about 4.5% made Indonesia one of the least affected G20 economies.

**Philippines.** The Maguindanao massacre in November 2009 killed 58 people, including 32 journalists. Benigno Aquino III was elected president in May 2010.

### Korean peninsula and Central Asia–Caucasus

**Korean peninsula.** Lee Myung-bak took office in South Korea in 2008. He faced mass protests over US beef imports, took a harder line toward the North and hosted the Seoul G20. North Korea tested a second nuclear device in May 2009 and carried out a disastrous currency redenomination in late 2009.

**Kyrgyzstan.**
- President Kurmanbek Bakiyev was overthrown on 7–8 April 2010, with at least 84–85 killed, and Roza Otunbayeva led an interim government.
- In June 2010 ethnic violence in Osh and Jalal-Abad killed at least 426 people by official data, or about 470 by the later Kyrgyzstan Inquiry Commission's estimate. Roughly three-quarters of the dead were Uzbek, and up to about 400,000 people were displaced for a time.
- A June referendum created a parliamentary system.

**Caucasus.** Georgia (Saakashvili) lost effective control of Abkhazia and South Ossetia. Armenia and Turkey signed normalization protocols in October 2009, but ratification stalled.

## IV. Thematic Sections

### Knowledge, science, and technology

Big science included CERN's Large Hadron Collider, the Kepler telescope, Japan's Hayabusa, China's Chang'e missions and India's Chandrayaan-1. In the life sciences, a draft Neanderthal genome showed interbreeding with modern humans (Max Planck Institute, Leipzig, May 2010), a cell ran on a synthetic genome (2010), and genome-sequencing costs collapsed, with China's BGI (Shenzhen) becoming a major sequencing center. Graphene, smartphones, cloud computing and the Tianhe-1A supercomputer marked materials and computing.

In 2010 the Belarusian security firm VirusBlokAda first identified the Stuxnet worm, which targeted industrial control systems; no one acknowledged authorship. Technique from the Global South included Kenyan mobile money, Indian biometric identity, the low-cost Tata Nano car (2009), Chinese scale in solar manufacturing and Brazilian ultra-deep-water drilling.

### Health and population

**H1N1.** WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on 25 April 2009, a Phase 6 pandemic on 11 June 2009, and the post-pandemic period in August 2010.
- At least 18,449 laboratory-confirmed deaths had been reported by August 2010, an acknowledged undercount.
- The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated 8,870–18,300 US deaths between April 2009 and April 2010.
- China reported about 127,500 confirmed cases and 799 deaths by March 2010.
- The Council of Europe and other critics questioned pharmaceutical influence over the pandemic declaration, and a WHO review was under way.
- Poorer countries received vaccines late.

**Other diseases.** Cholera struck Zimbabwe (2008–09) and Haiti (from October 2010). Malaria and tuberculosis burdens remained heavy.

**HIV/AIDS.** Treatment expanded through the US President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) and the Global Fund. South African policy shifted sharply under Zuma and health minister Aaron Motsoaledi. Two trials showed partial protection: the South African-led CAPRISA 004 trial of a tenofovir gel (July 2010), and the iPrEx trial of oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (November 2010).

**Health reform.** China announced a three-year, RMB 850 billion health reform in 2009, and the US passed insurance reform. Ageing advanced in Japan, Europe, Russia, South Korea and increasingly China, while large youth cohorts grew in Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and the Arab world.

### Security and conflict

**Interstate or near-interstate crises:**
- Russia–Georgia (2008).
- Gaza (2008–09).
- Korean clashes (2010).
- India–Pakistan tension after Mumbai. On 26–29 November 2008, ten Lashkar-e-Taiba gunmen arriving by sea from Karachi killed 166 people. The one captured alive, Ajmal Kasab, was convicted in May 2010.
- Thai–Cambodian clashes near the Preah Vihear temple.

**Civil wars and insurgencies** ran in Afghanistan, northwest Pakistan, Iraq, Sri Lanka (ended 2009), Somalia, Yemen, Darfur and Chad, eastern Congo, the Lord's Resistance Army's Central African borderlands, the southern Philippines, India's Maoist belt, Colombia and the North Caucasus. They also included Mexico's cartel conflict, where by the government's own count killings rose sharply after the military deployment.

**Jihadist networks** included al-Qaeda's core in Pakistan, AQAP, al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb (AQIM) in the Sahel and al-Shabaab.

**Military technique.** Somali piracy drew the largest multinational naval deployment of the period, including China's first distant-seas naval operations. Armed drones were in use, as were cyber operations (Stuxnet; US Cyber Command created in 2010).

**Arms control.** New START, the April 2010 Nuclear Security Summit, and a consensus final document at the May 2010 review conference of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT).

### Culture and society

**Social media in politics.** Social media entered political life under very different systems. They were visible in Iran's and Moldova's 2009 protests. In China, weibo microblogs launched in 2009–10 amid tightening censorship, and Facebook, Twitter and YouTube were blocked from 2009. In March 2010 Google moved its Chinese search service to Hong Kong after a cyber-intrusion and a censorship dispute.

**Prestige events by rising powers.** The Beijing Olympics (2008), the Shanghai Expo (2010, which drew 73.08 million visitors over 184 days and broke the attendance record set by Osaka's Expo 1970, according to the organizers and the Bureau International des Expositions), South Africa's World Cup and India's Commonwealth Games (both 2010). Future events were awarded to Brazil (2014 World Cup, 2016 Olympics), Russia (2018 World Cup) and Qatar (2022 World Cup).

**Religious currents.** Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity grew in Africa, Latin America and Asia, and religion revived in China under state management. Political Islam was contested, from Turkey's AKP to Iran's clerical-state debates to jihadist violence. The Catholic Church faced abuse scandals (in Ireland, the Ryan and Murphy reports of 2009), and anti-immigration politics grew in Europe, including Switzerland's 2009 minaret ban and gains by the Netherlands' Party for Freedom (PVV) in 2010.

**Culture and prizes.** Bollywood and Korean popular culture were spreading across Asia, and Nigeria's Nollywood was a major film producer. Literature Nobels went to Herta Müller (2009) and Mario Vargas Llosa (2010). Peace Nobels went to Obama (2009) and Liu Xiaobo (2010); Beijing urged other governments to boycott the 2010 ceremony.

## V. Conclusion

At the end of 2010 the defining fact was a North Atlantic financial crisis. The world had absorbed it without collapsing into protectionism, but at the price of heavy public debt in advanced economies and a credit and investment boom in China. China, India, Brazil, Indonesia and much of Sub-Saharan Africa kept growing through the shock, which contemporaries widely read as a shift of economic weight toward Asia and the "emerging" world. New forums institutionalized a wider club without displacing US military primacy or the dollar.

**The live tensions and open possibilities, as they stood:**
- Could the euro area stabilize its periphery without fiscal union or default?
- Could China rebalance toward consumption and contain local-government debt before its 2012 transition?
- Would the US recovery strengthen under a divided Congress, and would quantitative easing provoke currency conflicts?
- Would renewed food-price rises destabilize import-dependent North Africa and the Middle East, where Tunisian protests were spreading and succession questions hung over Egypt, Libya and Yemen?
- How would Sudan's referendum proceed?
- Would the Korean escalation continue as the North's succession advanced?
- Would the Afghan surge change the course of the insurgency?
- Would the Côte d'Ivoire standoff end peacefully?
- Would climate talks after Cancún produce binding commitments?

Contemporaries argued each of these both ways. None had a known outcome.
