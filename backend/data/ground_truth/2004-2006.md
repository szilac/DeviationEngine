# The State of the World, 2004–2006: A Ground-Truth Reference

## I. Executive Summary

The years 2004 through 2006 form a distinct interval in world affairs: a period of unusually strong and broadly shared global economic growth, powered by surging demand from China and other large developing economies and accompanied by a sharp, sustained rise in oil and commodity prices; a period of acute insecurity centered on the Iraq War and a widening arc of nuclear and terrorism-related crises; and a period in which the balance of the international system was visibly, if gradually, shifting away from unquestioned American and Western predominance toward a more genuinely multipolar distribution of economic and political weight.

The world economy grew at roughly 5% in 2004 — the strongest expansion in a generation — before moderating in 2005–2006 to a pace the UN described as "about 3 per cent during 2006," which it noted was "the same as the average of the past decade." These were preliminary estimates at the time. The United States remained the single largest economy and, in the UN's words, "the main engine of global economic growth," but its growth was increasingly financed by borrowing from abroad, producing record current-account and fiscal deficits that were widely debated as a systemic fragility. China's economy expanded by roughly 10% a year; its nominal GDP surpassed that of France in 2005 and the United Kingdom in 2006, and by end-February 2006 its foreign-exchange reserves reached about $853.6 billion, surpassing Japan's $850.1 billion for the first time to become the world's largest. India grew at 8–9%, and much of Africa and Latin America enjoyed their strongest growth in decades, buoyed by high prices for oil, metals, and agricultural commodities.

Politically, the period was dominated by the deteriorating war in Iraq, which by early 2006 had descended into sectarian civil conflict following the bombing of the al-Askari shrine in Samarra; by an intensifying standoff over Iran's nuclear program under newly elected President Mahmoud Ahmadinejad; by North Korea's first nuclear test in October 2006; by the war between Israel and Hezbollah in Lebanon in the summer of 2006; and by the continuing humanitarian catastrophe in Darfur. Two extraordinary natural disasters — the Indian Ocean tsunami of December 2004 and Hurricane Katrina in August 2005 — shaped the period's sense of vulnerability and tested state and international response capacities. In Europe, the historic 2004 enlargement of the EU to 25 members was followed by a crisis of political confidence after French and Dutch voters rejected the proposed EU constitution in 2005. Across Latin America a "pink tide" of left-of-center governments consolidated. In technology and society, this was the moment of early "Web 2.0" — the founding of YouTube, the dominance of MySpace, the expansion of Facebook beyond universities — and of intense global anxiety about a possible H5N1 avian-influenza pandemic.

The overall state of the world in 2004–2006 can be characterized as one of confident, commodity-fueled economic expansion coexisting uneasily with deepening security disorder and a dawning recognition that the unipolar "American moment" of the 1990s was giving way to something more plural, contested, and uncertain.

## II. Global Overview

### Distribution of power and great-power relations

The United States under the second-term administration of President George W. Bush was, by any conventional measure, the world's preeminent power — militarily unmatched, at the center of the global financial system, and the anchor of NATO and East Asian alliances. Yet its authority was being eroded by the Iraq War, which had cost it international legitimacy after the failure to find weapons of mass destruction, the Abu Ghraib prisoner-abuse revelations of 2004, and mounting casualties. The July 2004 report of the U.S. bipartisan 9/11 Commission found no "collaborative operational relationship" between Saddam Hussein's government and al-Qaeda, undercutting a central rationale for the war.

China, under the leadership of President Hu Jintao and Premier Wen Jiabao, was the rising force of the period, though contemporaries debated how far and how fast its rise would go. Its double-digit growth, its accession-driven integration into the World Trade Organization (which it had joined in 2001), and its accumulation of foreign reserves made it an increasingly indispensable actor, while its demand for raw materials reshaped commodity markets worldwide. The Hu–Wen leadership shifted rhetoric toward "scientific development" and a "harmonious society," acknowledging the environmental degradation and inequality generated by breakneck growth.

Russia, under President Vladimir Putin, was in a phase of reconsolidation of state power at home and renewed assertiveness abroad, financed by soaring oil and gas revenues. The dismemberment of the Yukos oil company and the imprisonment of Mikhail Khodorkovsky signaled the subordination of the oligarchs and the rise of a state-dominated energy sector centered on Gazprom and Rosneft. The European Union, mid-expansion after admitting ten new members in 2004, was simultaneously the world's largest economic bloc and a polity in existential doubt after the constitutional referendums failed. Japan, under the reformist Prime Minister Junichiro Koizumi, enjoyed a modest economic recovery. India under Prime Minister Manmohan Singh emerged as a genuine growth story and an increasingly courted partner. Relations among these powers combined cooperation (the six-party talks on North Korea, coordinated pressure on Iran through the P5+1) with rivalry (energy competition, trade friction, disputes over the Iraq War).

### The global economy and exchange

This was a period of exceptionally strong, broad-based growth. The IMF's assumed average price of oil — a simple average of spot prices of U.K. Brent, Dubai, and West Texas Intermediate crude — rose from $37.76 a barrel in 2004 to $54.23 in 2005 and $61.75 in 2006.\[1\] West Texas Intermediate rose from roughly $40 in mid-2004 to over $75 by August 2006, an increase of some 86% over that span.\[2\] Contemporaries attributed the run-up to tight spare capacity, surging demand from China and India, geopolitical tension in the Middle East, a falling U.S. dollar, and financial speculation. The commodity boom extended to metals and some agricultural goods, benefiting exporters across Africa, Latin America, the Middle East, and Central Asia.

The United States remained the principal engine of global demand but ran large and growing "twin deficits." Central banks across Asia — the Bank of Japan, and the central banks of China, South Korea, Taiwan, and India — accumulated historically high dollar reserves, recycling their trade surpluses into U.S. assets in a pattern widely described at the time as "global imbalances." The U.S. Federal Reserve steadily raised interest rates through the period to contain inflation, which oil prices had pushed to an average of about 3.3% in 2005–2006. A pronounced housing and construction boom was underway across the United States and much of Europe; the IMF and other bodies discussed elevated house prices as a potential vulnerability, but at the time this was described as a boom and a matter of debate, not a certain bubble.

### Social and demographic conditions

Global demographic trends featured continued rapid population growth in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, aging populations in Japan and Europe, and large-scale migration flows — labor migration into the Gulf states and Western Europe, and remittance flows back to South Asia, the Philippines, Latin America, and North Africa. Japan carried by far the largest government debt among industrial nations (about 160% of GDP) alongside a rapidly aging population. Poverty was falling in several regions: China lifted tens of millions out of poverty, India's expansion reduced deprivation unevenly, and Latin America's pink-tide governments used commodity revenues to fund cash-transfer and social programs that measurably reduced inequality in Brazil, Argentina, Bolivia, and elsewhere.

### Environment and natural events

Two catastrophic natural disasters defined the period. The Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami of 26 December 2004 — generated by a magnitude 9.1–9.3 megathrust rupture off Sumatra — killed approximately 228,000 people (the Tsunami Evaluation Coalition's final figure was 227,898; the UN Office of the Special Envoy cited 229,866) across some 15 countries, with Indonesia's Aceh province worst hit (most estimates of deaths there range from about 130,000 to 170,000), followed by Sri Lanka (over 30,000), India (over 10,000), and Thailand (over 5,000, including some 2,000 foreign tourists). Roughly 1.7 million people were displaced. The disaster prompted the creation of an Indian Ocean tsunami warning system in 2005.

Hurricane Katrina struck the U.S. Gulf Coast on 29 August 2005 as a Category 3 storm, causing catastrophic flooding in New Orleans after the failure of the levee system inundated some 80% of the city. Damage was estimated at roughly $125 billion, making it the costliest hurricane in U.S. history\[3\] to that point; the death toll is variously reported between about 1,200 and 1,836, with the National Hurricane Center attributing 1,836 fatalities and later Louisiana state analyses revising the direct toll toward roughly 1,170–1,464. The federal government's slow and disorganized response became a major domestic political scandal. Other significant disasters of the period included the October 2005 Kashmir earthquake.

Climate-change discourse in this period stood at a particular threshold. The Kyoto Protocol entered into force on 16 February 2005, after Russia's ratification brought it over the required threshold; the United States remained outside it. Public awareness of global warming rose sharply, culminating within the period in the 2006 release of the documentary film *An Inconvenient Truth*, featuring former U.S. Vice President Al Gore. Climate change was an established scientific and increasingly political concern, but not yet the central organizing issue of global politics it would later become.

### Flashpoints of the period

Five genuine flashpoints, judged from the vantage of 2004–2006 itself, stand out. First, Iraq, which moved from insurgency toward full sectarian civil war after the February 2006 Samarra shrine bombing. Second, Iran's nuclear program, which after Ahmadinejad's 2005 election and the resumption of enrichment triggered IAEA referral to the UN Security Council and the first sanctions resolution (1737) in December 2006. Third, North Korea, which conducted its first nuclear test on 9 October 2006. Fourth, the Israeli–Palestinian and Lebanese theater, encompassing Arafat's death, Israel's 2005 disengagement from Gaza, Hamas's January 2006 electoral victory, and the July–August 2006 Israel–Hezbollah war. Fifth, Darfur, where a campaign of mass killing and displacement continued despite an underpowered African Union monitoring mission.

### Emerging forces

Several forces that were small or nascent in 2004–2006 had visible room to grow, as a contemporary might have judged. China's economic trajectory was the most obvious: its share of global manufacturing and trade was climbing steeply. Social media and user-generated content were emerging: YouTube was founded in 2005, MySpace was the dominant social network, and Facebook was expanding beyond its original college base. Renewable energy was drawing new attention amid high fossil-fuel prices and Kyoto's entry into force. Groupings of large emerging economies (the "BRIC" concept, coined by a Goldman Sachs analyst in 2001) were discussed as an analytical category rather than an institutionalized bloc. India's information-technology and services sector was maturing into a global force.

### Systemic fragilities

Contemporary observers identified several systemic fragilities. Energy dependence and the vulnerability of the global economy to oil-price shocks were paramount. The U.S. current-account and fiscal deficits, and the associated global imbalances, were widely debated as a potential source of disorderly adjustment. Housing and credit conditions were discussed, with some analysts warning about elevated asset prices while others emphasized continued strength. The threat of an avian-influenza (H5N1) pandemic was treated as a first-order global health risk. And transnational terrorism — following the Madrid train bombings of March 2004 and the London bombings of July 2005 — remained a central preoccupation of Western and many non-Western governments alike.

## III. Major Powers and Regional Blocs

### United States

Domestically, President George W. Bush won a second term in November 2004, defeating Senator John Kerry in an election in which the Iraq War was a central issue. His second term was quickly buffeted by the flawed federal response to Hurricane Katrina, rising Iraq casualties, and declining approval of the war; by the November 2006 midterm elections public opinion had turned sharply against the administration's Iraq policy. The late-2006 Iraq Study Group, co-chaired by James A. Baker III and Lee Hamilton, judged the situation "grave and deteriorating." Economically, the United States combined solid growth with a housing and consumption boom, rising interest rates, and record external deficits. Key figures beyond the president included Vice President Dick Cheney, Secretaries of State Colin Powell (first term) and Condoleezza Rice (second term), and Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld. Barack Obama, elected to the U.S. Senate in 2004, was a first-term senator during this period.

### China

Under Hu Jintao and Wen Jiabao, China posted growth of roughly 9.5% in 2004, about 9.9–10% in 2005 (later revised upward), and continued high growth in 2006. Its trade surplus ballooned — net goods exports roughly tripled from about $32 billion in 2004 to over $100 billion in 2005 and then to about $177.5 billion in 2006 — and its foreign-exchange reserves reached roughly $853.6 billion by end-February 2006, surpassing Japan's $850.1 billion to become the world's largest. In July 2005 China abandoned its fixed dollar peg for a managed float, allowing modest renminbi appreciation (about 5.7% against the dollar from July 2005 to end-2006). The leadership's stated priorities shifted toward addressing rural poverty, environmental degradation, and inequality under the banners of "scientific development" and the "harmonious society," embodied in the 11th Five-Year Program (2006–2010). China's export success generated trade friction with the United States and the EU, especially over textiles.

### European Union and major member states

The EU's historic enlargement on 1 May 2004 admitted ten new members — Cyprus, the Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia, and Slovenia — extending the Union to 25 states and reunifying much of the continent after the Cold War. But the momentum was broken in 2005 when French voters rejected the proposed Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe on 29 May (about 55% against, on roughly 69% turnout) and Dutch voters followed on 1 June (about 61.6% against), ending the ratification process and triggering a "period of reflection" and a crisis of confidence. Germany, under a grand coalition led by Angela Merkel from late 2005, and France, under a weakened President Jacques Chirac, were the principal member states, with the United Kingdom under Tony Blair a close U.S. ally on Iraq. The euro area grew modestly.

### Russia

Putin's Russia used windfall energy revenues to repay foreign debt, build reserves, and reassert state control over strategic sectors. The Yukos affair — the tax-driven bankruptcy of the company (finalized in 2006) and the imprisonment of Mikhail Khodorkovsky — became emblematic of the new rules for oligarchs, and state-controlled Rosneft and Gazprom expanded dramatically. The September 2004 Beslan school siege, in which hundreds died, followed a series of terrorist attacks and prompted Putin to centralize political power further. Russia launched National Priority Projects in health, education, housing, and agriculture in 2005, and the English-language channel Russia Today was created that year. Poverty fell substantially (from around 38% in 1998 to under 10% by 2004 on official measures), even as inflation remained a concern, dropping from about 20% in 2000 to about 9% in 2006.

### Japan and India

Japan under Koizumi enjoyed an export-led recovery, posting real GDP growth of about 4.4% in 2004, while still grappling with deflation and enormous public debt. India under Prime Minister Manmohan Singh and the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance coalition, elected in 2004, accelerated to about 9.5% growth in 2005–06, driven by services, information technology, and rising investment. The period saw high growth combined with high food and fuel inflation; landmark policy included the launch of the National Rural Employment Guarantee scheme in 2006. Key figures included Congress president Sonia Gandhi and Finance Minister P. Chidambaram.

### The Middle East: Iraq, Iran, and the Levant

Iraq's trajectory dominated the region. After the June 2004 transfer of sovereignty to an interim government under Ayad Allawi, the war saw the bloody Second Battle of Fallujah in November 2004, elections in January and December 2005 that produced a Shia-led government and a new constitution, and then, after the 22 February 2006 bombing of the al-Askari (Golden) Mosque in Samarra, a descent into large-scale sectarian killing between Sunni and Shia militias. Casualty estimates were and remain contested: one widely cited (and disputed) 2006 study estimated more than 650,000 excess deaths since the 2003 invasion, while other tallies were far lower; U.S. military deaths passed 1,000 by the November 2004 election and surpassed 3,000 in early 2007. Abu Musab al-Zarqawi, leader of al-Qaeda in Iraq, was killed by a U.S. airstrike in June 2006; Saddam Hussein was executed in December 2006.

Iran emerged as a central concern after the June 2005 election of President Mahmoud Ahmadinejad. Iran resumed uranium conversion in August 2005; the IAEA Board found Iran in non-compliance with its safeguards agreement in September 2005 and voted (27–3) to report the matter to the UN Security Council in February 2006; in April 2006 Ahmadinejad announced Iran had enriched uranium to about 3.5% using a 164-centrifuge cascade at Natanz. The Security Council passed Resolution 1696 in July 2006 demanding suspension, and Resolution 1737 in December 2006 imposing the first sanctions.

In the Palestinian territories, the death of Yasser Arafat on 11 November 2004 opened a transition: Mahmoud Abbas was elected Palestinian Authority president in January 2005 with about 60% of the vote; Israel unilaterally disengaged from Gaza in 2005; and in January 2006 the Islamist movement Hamas won a surprise majority (74 of 132 seats) in Palestinian legislative elections, with Ismail Haniyeh becoming prime minister, prompting an international aid boycott and intensifying the Fatah–Hamas rift. In Lebanon, the February 2005 assassination of former prime minister Rafik Hariri triggered the "Cedar Revolution" and the withdrawal of Syrian forces. In July–August 2006, a 34-day war between Israel and Hezbollah killed over 1,000 Lebanese (the majority civilians, per Lebanese government and Human Rights Watch accounts; Human Rights Watch documented at least 1,109 Lebanese deaths) and about 160 Israelis (including 43 civilians), displaced roughly a million Lebanese, and ended inconclusively with UN Security Council Resolution 1701.

### Africa: the African Union and major states

Sub-Saharan Africa experienced strong growth in this period, averaging around 5–6% annually, driven by high commodity prices and, in some cases, improved governance. The dominant crisis was Darfur, in western Sudan, where from 2003 the government and allied Janjaweed militias waged a campaign against non-Arab populations; estimates of the dead ranged widely — around 200,000 for 2003–2005 in many accounts, with some later estimates of up to 300,000 or more, figures the Sudanese government contested — and roughly 2.7 million were displaced. The United States (under Secretary of State Colin Powell in September 2004) termed the violence genocide, while the UN and African Union used the term "crimes against humanity" but declined to call it genocide; an underfunded African Union Mission in Sudan monitored a fragile and short-lived 2006 Darfur Peace Agreement, and the UN Security Council referred the situation to the International Criminal Court in March 2005.

Other major developments included the January 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement ending the long north–south Sudanese civil war; the death of southern Sudanese leader John Garang in a July 2005 helicopter crash; and the November 2005 election of Ellen Johnson Sirleaf of Liberia as Africa's first elected female head of state (inaugurated 16 January 2006). The July 2005 G8 summit at Gleneagles, Scotland, coinciding with the "Make Poverty History" campaign and the Live 8 concerts, pledged to double aid to Africa by 2010 and launched the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative, which cancelled the multilateral debts of qualifying Heavily Indebted Poor Countries; by 2006, 14 sub-Saharan countries had reached the initiative's "completion point." Nigeria and South Africa were the continent's principal economic and political powers.

### Latin America: the pink tide

Latin America was marked by the consolidation of a "pink tide" of left-of-center governments, riding the commodity boom and a popular reaction against 1990s neoliberalism. By 2005–2006 leftist leaders governed much of South America: Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in Brazil\ (elected 2002, re-elected 2006), Néstor Kirchner in Argentina, Hugo Chávez in Venezuela, Ricardo Lagos and then Michelle Bachelet in Chile (Bachelet elected 2006 as Chile's first woman president), Tabaré Vázquez in Uruguay (2004), Evo Morales in Bolivia (elected December 2005 as the country's first indigenous president), and Rafael Correa in Ecuador and Daniel Ortega in Nicaragua (both elected 2006). Analysts distinguished a moderate, market-friendly social-democratic left (Lula, Bachelet) from a more radical, nationalist, statist current (Chávez, Morales). Social spending and cash-transfer programs such as Brazil's Bolsa Família\ reduced poverty and inequality across the region.

### Southeast Asia and ASEAN

Southeast Asia was a region of both democratic advance and instability. Indonesia held its first-ever direct presidential election in 2004, won by Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono, who defeated incumbent Megawati Sukarnoputri in a September 2004 runoff with about 60.6% of the vote and was inaugurated on 20 October 2004. In the aftermath of the tsunami, his government reached the landmark Helsinki Memorandum of Understanding on 15 August 2005, ending the roughly three-decade Aceh (GAM) insurgency, mediated by former Finnish president Martti Ahtisaari, with an EU–ASEAN Aceh Monitoring Mission overseeing implementation. Indonesian growth was about 5.0% in 2004, 5.7% in 2005, and 5.5% in 2006, and Yudhoyono cut costly fuel subsidies in October 2005.

Thailand slid from Prime Minister Thaksin Shinawatra's landslide re-election in February 2005 (his Thai Rak Thai party winning 377 of 500 seats) into political crisis, an annulled April 2006 snap election (voided by the Constitutional Court in May 2006), and finally a military coup on 19 September 2006 led by Army Commander-in-Chief General Sonthi Boonyaratglin while Thaksin was at the UN General Assembly in New York — Thailand's first coup in 15 years; retired General Surayud Chulanont was installed as interim prime minister. In the Philippines, President Gloria Macapagal Arroyo, re-elected in 2004, survived the 2005 "Hello Garci" wiretap scandal and multiple impeachment attempts, and declared a state of national emergency in February 2006. Vietnam, growing at roughly 8% a year under doi moi reforms (official figures of 8.4% in 2005 and about 8.17% in 2006), concluded WTO accession negotiations in 2006 (the WTO General Council approved its accession on 7 November 2006; it formally became the 150th member on 11 January 2007) and hosted the APEC summit in Hanoi in November 2006.

At the institutional level, ASEAN's ten members (including Myanmar/Burma, admitted 1997) advanced regional integration: leaders signed the Kuala Lumpur Declaration on the establishment of an ASEAN Charter on 12 December 2005 and convened the inaugural East Asia Summit in Kuala Lumpur on 14 December 2005, bringing together the ten ASEAN states plus Australia, China, India, Japan, South Korea, and New Zealand, with Russia attending as a guest. Myanmar remained under the military State Peace and Development Council led by Senior General Than Shwe (with General Soe Win as prime minister); the junta abruptly moved the administrative capital from Yangon to the newly built Naypyidaw on 6 November 2005 (the name publicly revealed on Armed Forces Day, 27 March 2006), and opposition leader and Nobel laureate Aung San Suu Kyi remained under house arrest throughout the period, her detention repeatedly extended.

## IV. Thematic Sections

### Knowledge, science, and technology

This was a formative period for what was then being called "Web 2.0" — the shift toward user-generated content, social networking, and interactive web services. YouTube was founded in February 2005 by three former PayPal employees (Chad Hurley, Steve Chen, and Jawed Karim) and grew explosively, delivering some 100 million video views a day by July 2006; Google agreed to acquire it in November 2006 for $1.65 billion. MySpace was the dominant social network, acquired by News Corporation for $580 million in July 2005 and becoming the most-visited U.S. website by mid-2006. Facebook, launched as "TheFacebook" in 2004 and renamed in 2005, expanded from Harvard to other universities and, in 2006, opened registration to the general public and introduced its News Feed. Twitter launched in 2006. The broader technology landscape was defined by feature phones and early smartphones such as the BlackBerry and Palm Treo, the ongoing transition from dial-up to broadband internet, and the pre-app-store mobile ecosystem.

In the life sciences, stem-cell research was a major scientific and ethical battleground, entangled in U.S. political debates over federal funding of embryonic stem-cell work. The field was rocked by a major scandal when the celebrated South Korean researcher Hwang Woo-suk was found in late 2005 to have fabricated landmark claims of having cloned human embryonic stem cells. In astronomy, the International Astronomical Union in August 2006 adopted a formal definition of "planet" that reclassified Pluto as a "dwarf planet," a widely publicized scientific decision. Avian-influenza research intensified as H5N1 spread. Space activity continued with the International Space Station, NASA's Mars rovers, and China's growing crewed space program.

### Health and population

The most acute global health anxiety of the period was the possibility that the H5N1 avian-influenza virus would mutate into a form capable of efficient human-to-human transmission and spark a pandemic. WHO-confirmed human H5N1 cases rose from 45 (with 32 deaths) at the start of 2005 to 142 (with 74 deaths) a year later, spreading beyond Vietnam and Thailand to Cambodia, Indonesia, China, and Turkey, while poultry outbreaks spread westward across Central Asia into Russia, Kazakhstan, Turkey, and Romania. The case-fatality rate among confirmed human cases exceeded 50%, and a WHO survey named avian influenza the number-one health concern of 2005; governments stockpiled the antiviral oseltamivir (Tamiflu) and drafted pandemic-preparedness plans.

The HIV/AIDS pandemic remained one of the gravest global health crises, with sub-Saharan Africa the epicenter; the period saw expanding but still inadequate access to antiretroviral therapy, supported by mechanisms such as the Global Fund and the U.S. PEPFAR program launched in 2003. Malaria and tuberculosis continued to impose heavy tolls in the developing world. The tsunami and Katrina both raised acute concerns about waterborne disease in their immediate aftermaths.

### Security and conflict

Beyond Iraq, the period's conflicts spanned the globe. Afghanistan saw the continuation of the NATO-led ISAF mission and a resurgent insurgency, though the war there was overshadowed by Iraq. Darfur remained the world's gravest humanitarian emergency. The 2006 Israel–Hezbollah war in Lebanon, North Korea's October 2006 nuclear test, and the Iran nuclear standoff each carried escalation risk. Terrorism was a defining security theme: the Madrid train bombings of 11 March 2004 (nearly 200 killed) and the London transit bombings of 7 July 2005 (52 killed) brought mass-casualty jihadist violence to major European cities, while attacks also struck Bali (2005), Egyptian Sinai resorts, Amman, and Mumbai (the July 2006 commuter-train bombings). Russia's North Caucasus remained violent, epitomized by the Beslan school siege of September 2004. Sri Lanka's civil war between the government and the Tamil Tigers reignited in 2005–2006 after the collapse of a ceasefire. Nepal saw a Maoist insurgency and a 2006 popular movement that curtailed the monarchy.

### Culture and society

Culturally, the period was shaped by the accelerating digitization of media and the early democratization of content creation through blogging and video-sharing. Religion remained a powerful force in world affairs: Pope John Paul II died in April 2005 after a long pontificate and was succeeded by Pope Benedict XVI; debates over the role of Islam in politics intensified across the Middle East and in Europe, where the 2005 publication of cartoons depicting the Prophet Muhammad in a Danish newspaper triggered widespread protests across the Muslim world in early 2006. Globalization produced both integration and backlash, visible in the anti-neoliberal politics of Latin America's pink tide and in European anxieties about enlargement, immigration, and Turkey's potential EU accession that colored the constitutional referendums. Major global sporting events included the 2004 Athens Olympics and the 2006 FIFA World Cup in Germany. In popular culture, this was a period of transition from physical media toward digital distribution of music and video, with Apple's iPod and the iTunes Store reshaping the music industry.

## V. Conclusion

Viewed from within its own moment, the world of 2004–2006 was defined by a striking tension between economic optimism and geopolitical anxiety. The global economy was enjoying one of its strongest and most broadly shared expansions in decades, lifting hundreds of millions in China, India, and across the developing world, and filling the treasuries of commodity exporters from Russia to Latin America to Africa. High oil prices, booming housing and construction markets, abundant credit, and rising asset values created a pervasive sense of prosperity, even as thoughtful observers pointed to global imbalances, energy dependence, and elevated asset prices as potential vulnerabilities whose resolution remained genuinely uncertain.

At the same time, the international security order was visibly fraying. The Iraq War had become a sectarian civil conflict and a drain on American power and prestige; Iran and North Korea were pressing against the nuclear-nonproliferation regime; the Israeli–Palestinian conflict had entered a new and more fragmented phase; and Darfur stood as a reproach to the international community's capacity to prevent mass atrocity. The unipolar predominance the United States had enjoyed after the Cold War was, as contemporaries increasingly sensed, giving way to a more multipolar world in which China's rise, Russia's resurgence, India's emergence, and the EU's uncertain deepening all pointed toward a more contested distribution of power.

The live possibilities as they stood in mid-2006 were genuinely open. It was unclear whether Iraq could be stabilized, whether Iran's nuclear program could be halted diplomatically, whether the EU could recover its integrative momentum, whether the commodity boom would continue or reverse, whether an avian-influenza pandemic would materialize, and how far and how fast China would rise. Contemporaries did not know — and could not have known — which of the period's many emerging forces would prove decisive. What they could observe was a world of vigorous growth, deepening disorder, and a shifting balance of power, poised between the certainties of the post-Cold-War era and an unwritten future.
